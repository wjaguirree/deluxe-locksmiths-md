Commercial Lock Installation

Commercial-grade locks specified and installed for storefronts, offices and back-of-house doors. Available across Northeast Maryland & Delaware.

Call (443) 545-2775 Request service

A bad commercial lock job usually shows up where it hurts most: a door that doesn't line up right, a latch that drags, a lock that wears out too soon, or a key control setup that leaves the wrong people with access. For a storefront, office, or back-of-house entry, that kind of problem slows staff down and weakens the door you're relying on every day. The lock has to fit the door, the frame, the hardware, and the way the building is used.

We install commercial-grade locks for businesses across Cecil County, Harford County, and Kent County, with most work handled on site where the door is already in service. That matters when you're dealing with hollow metal doors, wood storefronts, replacement hardware, or an entry that needs to work with existing closers, panic hardware, or keyed access. On a place like a shop along Main Street or an office off Pulaski Highway, the right lock choice is only part of the job. The rest is getting the strike, latch, and alignment right so the door closes cleanly and holds up under daily use.

If you need a new lock installed, a worn unit replaced, or hardware matched to a door that's giving you trouble, we can handle it without turning the job into a bigger disruption than it has to be.

When you actually need this

Commercial lock installation makes sense when the problem is bigger than a worn key or a sticky latch. If you're taking over a tenant space, changing the use of a building, replacing hollow office doors with solid-core doors, or trying to bring several entrances under one key plan, new hardware is usually the right move. It also comes up when the existing lock was never a good match for the door. A light-duty cylindrical lock on a busy employee entrance, for example, tends to loosen, sag, and chew up the latch over time. On aluminum storefront doors, older narrow-stile hardware often reaches a point where parts are scarce, the keyway is sloppy, and the whole setup needs to be replaced with the right mortise cylinder, deadbolt, or Adams Rite style hardware instead of patched again.

You also need installation when the door's function has changed. A public-facing entrance may need a storeroom function lock so staff can exit freely while the outside stays secure. A rear service door may need a commercial deadbolt paired with a door closer and proper strike reinforcement because deliveries, carts, and repeated traffic have started pulling the frame out of alignment. Offices that have outgrown basic key control may need a restricted keyway or a keyless entry setup rather than another copy of the same old key floating around. In mixed-use buildings, it's common to find a mismatch of knob locks, levers, deadlatches, and improvised fixes. At that point, standardizing the hardware is often cleaner and more reliable than chasing one failure after another.

On the other hand, not every lock problem calls for a full replacement. If the hardware is the correct grade and the core is still sound, commercial lock repair may be the better answer. A door that won't latch can be a closer issue, a bad strike alignment, loose hinges, or frame movement rather than a failed lock. If security is fine but key control isn't, rekeying usually solves the problem without changing the whole trim set. If employees are propping a door because it's hard to open, the lock may be blamed when the real problem is a binding door closer or panic device. And if you need one manager key over several rooms, a master key system may be the smarter service than swapping every lock on the property.

The practical test is this: install new hardware when the existing lock is the wrong type, the wrong grade, no longer serviceable, or no longer fits how the door is used. Repair or rekey when the body of the lock is still worth keeping and the issue is wear, key control, or adjustment. In older properties around Elkton, especially on storefront doors and back-of-house openings that have seen years of piecemeal fixes, the best result often comes from looking at the whole opening: door, frame, closer, strike, latch, and traffic pattern. That's how you decide whether a fresh installation will actually solve the problem or just cover it for a while.

How the work is done

The job starts with the door, not the lock in a box. Before any hardware is picked, the technician checks what kind of opening is there and how it's used through a normal day. A narrow aluminum storefront door takes different prep and hardware than a solid wood office door or a hollow metal back-of-house door. The frame condition matters just as much. If the latch side is worn, the strike is loose, or the door is sagging on the hinges, a new lock alone won't fix the problem. The handing of the door, the backset, cross-bore size, latch face dimensions, and door thickness all have to match. On glass-and-aluminum storefronts, the stile width and existing prep often decide whether a mortise cylinder setup, Adams Rite style deadlatch, or hook bolt is the right fit. On interior offices, cylindrical levers are common, but some openings call for mortise locks because they hold up better under heavier traffic and offer more functions in one case body.

Once the opening is evaluated, the lock function is matched to the use of that specific door. A front customer entrance may need a storeroom function, an office function, or a classroom-style function depending on who needs to control entry and whether the inside lever must always allow egress. A stockroom or employee entrance may need a deadbolt paired with a lever lock, or a single commercial deadlatch if the door and frame are built for it. If the business wants key control, that decision is made before pins are set, because the cylinder may need to be tied into an existing master key system or built on restricted keyway hardware. If the opening is part of a fire-rated assembly, the replacement hardware has to stay compatible with that rating. Door closers, weatherstrip, panic hardware, and electric strikes can also affect what will fit and work correctly.

Installation itself is careful layout and clean prep. If the door already has the right holes and cutouts, old hardware is removed, the prep is checked for damage, and any wallowed-out screw holes or misaligned strikes are corrected before the new lock goes on. If the prep is wrong, drilling and mortising are done to the manufacturer template so the chassis sits square and the latch lines up with the strike. Through-bolts are preferred where the hardware allows it because they hold better on high-use doors than short wood or sheet-metal screws. Cylinder length is set so the face sits properly without excess projection. On storefront hardware, the tailpiece length, cam type, and set screw engagement all matter. The strike is positioned so the latch enters cleanly without rubbing, and on pairs of doors the technician checks clearances to make sure one leaf isn't forcing the other out of alignment.

After the lock is mounted, the real test is operation under normal use. The door is opened and closed repeatedly to confirm latch throw, deadlocking, key rotation, lever return, and inside egress. Common failure points show up here: a closer pulling too hard, a warped door causing bind, a strike cut too shallow, a latch bevel facing the wrong way, or a cylinder that turns but doesn't fully retract the latch because the cam is mismatched. If needed, the strike is adjusted, the closer is tuned, or the cylinder is repinned so the keying matches the rest of the property. The goal is a lock that fits the opening, works consistently, and doesn't start wearing itself out because the door was ignored. That's true whether the work is for a small office near Elkton or a retail entrance off Pulaski Highway.

What it takes to do it properly

A proper commercial lock installation starts before any holes are drilled. The door, frame, traffic pattern, and the way the business actually uses the opening all matter. A narrow stile aluminum storefront door takes different hardware than a solid wood office door or a hollow metal back door. On some openings, a standard cylindrical lever lock is right. On others, the better answer is a mortise lock, a storefront deadbolt, or an interconnected lockset that ties latch and deadbolt functions together. You also need the right backset, door thickness, handing, latch face, strike type, and cylinder format. If the door is tied into an existing master key system, the new hardware has to match that plan instead of creating one more odd key that only works on one opening. The job goes wrong when someone treats every commercial door like a house front door and installs whatever happens to fit the cutout.

The equipment matters too, because commercial hardware punishes sloppy work. A clean installation takes proper jigs, hole saws, mortising tools, drill guides, latch face templates, accurate measuring tools, and fasteners suited to the door and frame material. On steel frames, the strike has to land where the latch and deadbolt actually engage with full support, not just catch the edge and wear out. On aluminum storefront doors, cylinder collars, Adams Rite style components, and the mounting method all have to be right or the hardware loosens up under daily use. Door closers, weatherstrip drag, sagging hinges, and frame spread also have to be checked because a lock can be perfectly good and still fail in service if the door doesn't close square or the latch won't line up with the strike.

Judgment is what separates a proper result from a bodged one. A good installation accounts for security, life safety, and daily convenience at the same time. A rear employee entrance may need a deadlocking latch and tighter key control. A customer-facing door may need panic hardware or to work with an existing closer so people aren't fighting the door all day. Double-cylinder hardware might look like extra security, but on the wrong opening it creates a serious egress problem. A keyed lever may be fine for a private office, while a storeroom function lock is often better for stock rooms or utility spaces. Keyless entry can make sense on some doors, but only if the door condition, power plan, and user traffic support it.

The difference between done right and done badly shows up after the installer leaves. A proper lock throws fully, retracts smoothly, and doesn't need to be lifted, slammed, or shoulder-pushed to work. The trim sits tight, the cylinder is centered, the strike is reinforced, and the key turns without binding. Screws aren't stripped into soft wood or left barely holding in an old frame. Existing problems are called out instead of covered up. On busy doors in places like Elkton or along Pulaski Highway, that matters because commercial hardware sees constant use. A bodged job may lock on day one, but it chews up latches, loosens through-bolts, cracks trim, and leaves staff dealing with a door that never works the same way twice.

Getting it right the first time

A commercial lock install goes sideways when the lock is treated like a part you swap, instead of part of a door system. A storefront narrow stile door takes different hardware than a solid core office door or a steel back-of-house door. If the prep is wrong, the cylinder can sit crooked, the latch may not throw cleanly, and the key will feel rough from day one. On aluminum storefront doors, the body of an Adams Rite style deadlatch or deadbolt has to match the existing cutout and backset, or you end up forcing hardware into a door that was never prepared for it. On wood or hollow metal doors, the problem is often a bored prep that is slightly off, which leads to a latch that rubs, binds, or fails to align with the strike under normal use.

A lot of second visits come from picking the wrong function, not bad workmanship. An office may need a storeroom function lock where the outside lever stays locked and always needs a key, while an interior hall door might need classroom or office function instead. Put the wrong function on a stockroom, and staff either prop the door open or start leaving keys in the cylinder. Install the wrong closer or ignore the existing closer, and the latch never seats fully unless the door is pulled shut by hand. Panic hardware has the same issue. A rim exit device on a pair of doors, for example, is only as reliable as the strike, door alignment, and coordinator setup around it. If those pieces are worn or out of adjustment, the new device gets blamed for a door problem that was already there.

Weather, traffic, and wear matter more than many people think. Around Elkton and up near Route 40, exterior commercial doors see temperature swing, dust, moisture, and a lot of opening cycles. That affects whether a standard cylindrical lock is the right choice or whether a grade 1 mortise lock, heavy-duty closer, continuous hinge, or armored strike makes more sense. A lock can be perfectly good and still fail early if the door sags, the frame shifts, or the latch is taking the hit instead of the closer controlling the close. For keyed systems, pinning also needs to be planned before installation. If a property wants future expansion into a master key system, that should be built in at the start, not patched together after tenants and managers already have keys in circulation.

Getting it right the first time means checking the whole opening before hardware is ordered or installed: door material, handing, backset, faceplate dimensions, latch projection, strike location, closer force, hinge wear, and how the door is actually used during a workday. It also means matching hardware to the job instead of forcing a one-size-fits-all lock onto every opening. For one business that may mean a restricted keyway on management doors, a lever set for employee areas, and keyless entry on a delivery entrance. For another, it may mean replacing a failing storefront deadlatch with the correct commercial-grade body and making sure the strike and door fit are corrected at the same time. That extra attention is what prevents callbacks for sticky keys, doors that won't latch, cylinders that loosen in the trim, and hardware that never should have been installed on that opening to begin with.

What to have ready

Before you call, it helps to know exactly which door needs the new lock and what that door is made of. A narrow aluminum storefront door takes different hardware than a solid wood office door, a hollow metal back door, or a glass door with a stile. If you can, check whether the door already has a cylindrical lever lock, a mortise lock, a deadbolt, a rim exit device, or a keypad. Also note what is going wrong now. Common problems include a loose lever, a latch that won't retract cleanly, a deadbolt that binds because the door has dropped, a panic bar that no longer latches, or a key that turns rough because the cylinder is worn. If the door is fire rated, that matters too, because the replacement hardware has to match the door's listing and the way it closes and latches.

It also helps to gather a few practical details from the site. Know how many keys or users the business needs, who should have access to which doors, and whether you want the new hardware to work on a master key system or stand alone. If you're replacing several locks, count the openings and note whether they are exterior, interior, shared, or restricted areas such as storage, offices, or back-of-house doors. A few clear photos are useful, especially close shots of the edge of the door, the lock face, the strike, and the handle set. If there is a door closer, electric strike, alarm contact, or keyless entry already tied into the opening, mention that up front. Parking and access can matter at a busy storefront near Main Street, because the work area may need to stay clear while the door is tested.

On the day, the first step is to confirm the opening, inspect the door and frame, and make sure the hardware choice fits the actual condition of the entrance. A good installation is not just swapping parts. The locksmith checks backset, bore size, cross bore and edge prep, handing, latch alignment, strike location, and whether the frame is tight and square. If the existing prep doesn't match the lock you want, the job may call for a wrap plate, a new strike, through-bolting, a mortise cylinder change, or a different grade of hardware that better suits traffic and wear. For a storefront on US 40, that can mean choosing hardware that stands up to repeated daily use and resists sagging or sloppy latch engagement.

Once the hardware is installed, the door is tested the way staff will actually use it: closed slowly, pushed shut, keyed from outside, operated from inside, and checked for clean latch throw and smooth return. If the job includes keyed alike cylinders, restricted key control, panic hardware, or a keypad, those functions are set up and verified before wrapping up. You should expect a clear handoff at the end: which keys operate which doors, how the lock should feel when it is working correctly, and what signs mean the door itself may need adjustment later. Having the right person on site to approve key access and answer questions avoids confusion after the new lock is in place.

Frequently asked questions

Can you install new commercial locks on my existing doors, or do the doors and frames need to be replaced too?

Often the existing door can stay, but the answer depends on its condition and how it was prepped. We look at the door material, backset, edge bore, frame alignment, and whether the closer or strike is already causing problems. If a door is sagging, split, or badly out of alignment, a new lock alone won't fix the issue. In some cases, modifying the prep is possible. In others, it creates a weaker or less reliable setup.

What kind of lock makes sense for a front entrance versus an office or stockroom door?

Those doors usually need different levels of control. A storefront entrance may need a narrow-stile setup, panic hardware, or a lock that works well with heavy daily traffic. An office door might need privacy and key control, while a stockroom or back-of-house door may need stricter restriction and stronger resistance to forced entry. The goal is to match the hardware to how the door is actually used, not just put the same lock on every opening.

Can new commercial locks be keyed to work with our current master key system?

Sometimes yes, sometimes no. It depends on the brand, keyway, cylinder format, and how your current system was built. If the existing setup still gives good key control and the hardware is in decent shape, new locks can often be pinned into that system. If the system has gotten messy over time, with too many exceptions or copied keys floating around, it's usually better to clean it up and rebuild the hierarchy instead of forcing new hardware into an outdated plan.

Do I need to worry about fire code, egress, or accessibility when changing commercial locks?

Yes, especially on customer-facing doors and employee exits. A lock choice can affect whether a door still allows proper egress, whether the hardware can be operated without tight grasping or twisting, and whether a fire-rated opening stays compliant. Double-cylinder deadbolts, the wrong lever trim, or field modifications to rated doors can create problems. If a door serves the public or is part of an exit path, the hardware should be chosen with code and daily use in mind.
