Commercial Locksmith Services

Master keys, panic hardware and access control that keep a business open, compliant and under control.

A storefront on W Main St can turn into a problem fast when a key won't turn, a latch drags, or the panic bar won't release cleanly. That kind of issue doesn't wait for a quiet day, and it usually shows up when the door has already taken a beating from daily use, weather, and too many hands on the hardware. We handle commercial lock installation, repair, rekeying, and storefront door lock repair for businesses that need the entrance to work the way it should and stay that way.

Across Cecil County, Harford County, and Kent County, we set up master key systems, keyless entry, door closers, and panic hardware for offices, shops, warehouses, and other commercial spaces. If you need one key to manage several doors, better control over who gets in, or hardware that keeps doors closing and latching properly, we can take care of it where the work is. When a lock is worn, a key is missing, or a door isn't meeting the way it should, the fix needs to be practical, secure, and built around how your business actually runs.

What this category covers

Commercial locksmith work is the part of the trade that deals with buildings people use for business every day: storefronts, offices, clinics, warehouses, churches, small industrial spaces, and mixed-use properties with staff-only doors in the back. The job is bigger than putting a key in a lock. It includes the lock itself, the door, the frame, the closer, the exit device, the cylinder, and the way people are supposed to move through the building. A front entrance might need a mortise lock with a thumbturn inside, a rear steel door might need a heavy-duty cylindrical lever set, and a pair of glass aluminum doors may rely on narrow-stile hardware with a hook bolt and overhead concealed closer. When one part is wrong, the whole opening suffers: doors drag, latches don't line up, closers leak, panic bars bind, and staff start propping doors open just to get through the day.

That's why the different services in this category overlap so often. A call for lock repair can turn out to be a door closer problem that's letting the door slam past the strike. A rekey after employee turnover may reveal a master key system that was added onto too many times and no longer gives clean separation between managers, maintenance, and general staff. Storefront door lock repair often includes correcting worn pivots, loose push paddles, misaligned thresholds, or a mortise cylinder that turns rough because the door is no longer hanging true. Panic hardware installation also isn't just about the bar on the door. It has to match the door width, the latch style, the occupancy needs, and the condition of the frame so the exit path works the way it should during daily use and during an inspection.

Master keys, rekeying, and keyless entry all deal with control, but they solve different problems. Rekeying changes who can use an existing lock when a key goes missing or staffing changes. A master key system organizes access levels across multiple doors so the person opening the stockroom is not automatically opening the office, alarm closet, or server room. Keyless systems shift that control from physical keys to codes, credentials, schedules, and audit trails. In a small office in Elkton, a keypad on one interior door may be enough. In a larger site off Pulaski Highway, it may make more sense to tie several openings together so access can be changed without chasing down copies of old keys.

What ties all of this together is that commercial hardware lives a hard life. A back door may cycle constantly with deliveries. A public entrance has to close securely over and over without becoming difficult for customers to use. A repair that ignores the frame or a new lock installed on a worn-out opening won't hold up well. So this category covers the full working set of commercial door security: installing the right grade of hardware, restoring failed parts, structuring who gets access to what, and making sure exit doors, closers, cylinders, levers, and storefront components operate as one system instead of a pile of separate pieces.

Choosing the right job

The right job starts with what the door is doing, not with a guess about the lock. If a key turns hard, sticks at certain points, or only works when you pull on the door, that often points to wear, bad alignment, or a sagging door rather than a cylinder that needs replacement. A storefront that binds at the threshold may need storefront door lock repair, pivot work, or a door closer adjustment so the latch lines up and fully seats. If the keyway is sloppy, the tailpiece is worn, or the latch retracts inconsistently, a commercial lock repair may be enough. If the hardware itself is light-duty residential grade on a business door, that's usually when commercial lock installation makes more sense than repeated repair. A proper job looks at the door, frame, latch, strike, closer, and traffic pattern as one system.

Rekeying and master key work solve different problems, and mixing them up wastes time. If an employee left with a key, a ring went missing, or a contractor still has access they shouldn't have, commercial lock rekeying is usually the direct fix when the existing hardware is sound. The cylinders are changed to a new key so you're not replacing every lock body on the property. A master key system is the better fit when access has become messy: too many copies floating around, managers carrying a janitor-sized ring, or staff opening spaces they don't need. In that case, the issue is key control and hierarchy. A tiered system can separate front office, stock room, utility areas, and exterior doors so the right people open the right doors and no more. If a lost master has exposed multiple openings, you may need both a rekey and a redesigned master system.

Exit hardware and closers are often chosen because of symptoms people write off as "just the door." A panic bar that rattles, dogged down improperly, fails to latch, or needs a shoulder shove to open is not just annoying; it can become a life-safety issue. That points to panic bar installation or repair with the correct device for the occupancy, door width, and latch style. If a fire-rated corridor door drifts open, slams, or stops short and sits ajar, the closer likely needs replacement or proper adjustment. On rear exits and busy side doors, a bad closer can create repeat lock complaints because the latch never meets the strike squarely. In older buildings around Elkton, it's common to find decent hardware on a door that's out of alignment, so choosing the right job means looking beyond the symptom at the key.

Keyless entry fits best when the problem is administrative, not mechanical. If managers are constantly chasing returned keys, changing staff codes on a shared alarm note, or trying to track who entered a side door, a keypad, fob, or access-control setup may be the cleaner answer than another round of copies. It's also worth considering when one opening needs an audit trail while the rest of the building still works well on keyed hardware. Not every door should be converted. A stockroom with heavy daily use may do well with electronic access, while a seldom-used mechanical room may be better left on a standard mortise cylinder under a master system. On a Route 40 storefront, for example, the front aluminum door might need hook-bolt and closer work first, then access control only if the business actually needs tighter user management. Choosing the right job means matching the service to the failure, the door type, and how the building is really used.

Standards and hardware

On commercial doors, the difference between hardware that lasts and hardware that turns into a service call usually starts with grade, door type, and traffic. A lock on a storage room that opens a few times a day is not asked to do the same job as a front entrance on a busy storefront in Elkton. For cylindrical locks, mortise locks, closers, exit devices, and hinges, ANSI/BHMA grades matter because they tell you how the hardware was tested for cycle count, strength, and abuse. Grade 1 is the heavy-duty end of the scale and belongs on entrances, school-type corridors, and doors that get hit with carts, deliveries, and constant use. Grade 2 has its place on lighter interior openings. The mistake is treating every commercial opening like an office closet, then wondering why levers sag, latches stop catching, or a closer starts leaking oil after regular use.

The hardware type matters just as much as the grade. A narrow stile aluminum storefront door usually calls for a different setup than a solid wood office door or a hollow metal rear exit. On storefronts, we often see mortise cylinders, hook bolts, Adams Rite style deadlatches, pivot issues, worn thresholds, and misalignment that keeps the latch from landing cleanly in the strike. On steel or wood doors, a full mortise lock body may be the right answer where strength and function matter more than keeping a slim profile. Cylindrical locks are common and useful, but they are not the cure for every opening. Panic hardware has its own trade-offs too: rim exit devices are straightforward on some doors, while vertical rod devices fit others better but demand better alignment and more maintenance if the top or bottom latching points drift out of adjustment.

Quality differences show up in the parts you do not notice until they fail. A commercial closer should have the right spring size and proper backcheck and sweep adjustment for the door, not just enough tension to make it shut. If the closer is undersized, the door drifts or stays ajar. If it is cranked too hard to overcome another problem, the door slams, shakes the frame, and wears out hinges and latch parts. The same goes for strikes, reinforcing plates, and fasteners. A good lock installed on a weak frame prep or loose strike will still give trouble. Door handing, latch backset, stile width, fire rating, and whether the opening needs electrified hardware all need to be checked before parts are chosen.

For master key systems and keyless entry, the standard is not just whether the key turns or the reader beeps. The system has to match how the business actually operates. A proper master key plan limits who can enter stock rooms, offices, and exterior doors without creating accidental cross-keying that causes headaches later. With keypad, credential, or managed access systems, the hardware has to work with the door condition already there. A sagging door on a loading side off Route 40 will make even good electrified hardware seem unreliable if the latch and strike are fighting each other. Good commercial locksmith work starts with the opening, the code requirements, and the traffic pattern, then selects hardware that fits the job instead of forcing the job to fit whatever was on the truck.

Across our service area

Commercial locksmith work changes from one property to the next, and it also changes from town to town across Cecil County, Harford County, and Kent County. A small office with three interior doors needs a very different setup than a restaurant with a rear delivery entrance, a retail floor, and staff turnover at the register. In one building, the right answer may be a conventional cylindrical lever lock with a restricted keyway and a simple two-level master key system. In another, it may be a narrow-stile storefront lock at the front, a storeroom function lock at stock areas, and keypad access at employee-only doors so management is not chasing down missing keys. Older buildings often bring wear, alignment drift, and mixed hardware from past repairs, while newer spaces tend to raise questions about access control, code compliance, and who should be able to enter which room.

Storefront work is one of the clearest examples of how conditions vary around the service area. Aluminum-framed glass doors take different hardware than a solid wood office door or a hollow metal rear exit. On some storefronts, the problem is a worn mortise cylinder, a loose paddle, or a hook bolt that no longer throws cleanly into the strike because the door has settled. On others, the issue is lower down: a bad pivot, a dragging threshold, or a closer that has lost control and now lets the door slam, bounce, or sit slightly open. Along Route 40, for example, retail and service spaces see steady foot traffic, and that repeated use shows up in sagging doors, loose trim, and panic devices that need proper adjustment rather than another temporary fix. The work has to account for the whole opening, not just the lock body.

Back-of-house and employee areas bring a different set of trade-offs. A shop owner may want every manager to carry one master key while keeping office, inventory, and utility rooms separated under sub-master levels. A clinic or professional office may need tighter key control, with patent-protected blanks or electronic credentials to limit untracked duplication. After staff turnover, rekeying is often the practical move when the hardware is still sound; when the lock is worn, sticky, or no longer suited to the door's use, replacement makes more sense. Exit doors need the same kind of judgment. A panic bar has to latch reliably, release properly, and match the occupancy and door width. If there is exterior trim, dogging, or alarmed egress tied into the opening, those details matter because they affect daily operation as much as life-safety compliance.

County lines matter less than building type, but the local mix of properties does shape the work. In older downtown blocks near Main Street, it is common to find a patchwork of legacy cylinders, obsolete keyways, and closers adjusted around long-standing door problems instead of corrected at the source. In light industrial spaces, warehouses, and service bays, the usual concerns are tougher hardware, better key hierarchy, and controlling who can access side doors, caged storage, and office areas without handing out more metal keys than necessary. Across the region, the common thread is practical control: doors that latch, closers that behave, exits that meet code, and key systems that match the way the business actually runs.

Working with us

When you call about a commercial lock problem, the first step is figuring out what kind of door and hardware you're dealing with, because a steel rear entry, a narrow-stile aluminum storefront, and an office lever set all fail in different ways. We'll ask what the door is doing wrong: not latching unless it's pulled hard, key turning but not retracting the bolt, panic bar sticking, closer leaking, keypad not accepting credentials, or a master key working where it shouldn't. We also ask how the opening is used. A side employee entrance that sees steady traffic needs a different approach than a stockroom door or a front glass door on Route 40. If there's been staff turnover, a lost key ring, or an inspection issue, that shapes the plan from the start.

On site, we don't just look at the cylinder and call it done. We check the whole opening: lock body, strike, hinges or pivots, door closer, frame condition, door alignment, and whether the latch is meeting the strike cleanly. A lot of commercial lock trouble starts with sag, binding, loose through-bolts, worn storefront pivots, or a closer that's out of adjustment and letting the door drift open. For panic hardware, we look at dogging, latch retraction, vertical rods if present, and whether the device is appropriate for the occupancy and the door. For key systems, we identify what keyway you have now, whether cylinders match across the building, and whether the existing setup has grown into a patchwork that's hard to control.

Before work starts, we talk through the trade-offs in plain terms. Rekeying makes sense when the hardware is still sound and key control is the main concern. Full replacement is usually better when a lock body is worn, a lever return is failing, a storefront mortise cylinder has been changed so many times the setup is inconsistent, or the door needs to meet a different level of use. With access control, we'll explain the difference between a standalone keypad, a fob-based system, and a managed setup tied to schedules and user permissions. For door closers, we look at arm style, closer size, and adjustment so the door shuts positively without slamming. For panic devices, we match the opening to rim, mortise, or concealed vertical rod hardware as the door allows.

Once the scope is set, the work is done with an eye toward reliability and handoff, not just getting the door to move again. Cylinders are pinned to the agreed keying plan, strikes are set so latches seat properly, closers are adjusted through the full swing, and electronic hardware is tested with the credentials or codes it will actually use. If it's a master key system, you'll know who should have access to what, and the key schedule is documented so future changes don't turn into guesswork. If it's a storefront repair, we make sure the hook bolt throws correctly, the thumbturn or key retracts as it should, and the door closes into the frame without drag. Before wrapping up, we go over what was corrected, what to watch for next, and any hardware that is still serviceable now but showing wear.

Frequently asked questions

Can you set up a master key system without giving every employee access to everything?

Yes. A master key system can be built in layers so each key only opens the doors tied to that role. For example, one key might open a front entry and office, while another also opens storage or utility rooms. The important part is planning the hierarchy before cylinders are pinned. If a building has grown over time, it may make sense to rework parts of the system so access is easier to manage and audit.

Should we rekey our business locks or replace the hardware entirely?

It depends on the condition of the lock, the type of keyway, and whether you want to change how access is managed. Rekeying is often the right move when keys have changed hands but the hardware is still solid. Replacement makes more sense when locks are worn, the door is unreliable, or you want to move to restricted keys, panic hardware, or keyless entry. A storefront lock that sticks or binds may also point to a door alignment issue, not just a cylinder problem.

Can panic bars and door closers be added to an older commercial door, or does the whole door need to be replaced?

Often they can be added, but not every door is a good candidate. The door material, frame condition, backset, width, and how the door seals all matter. Fire-rated openings need extra care because the wrong hardware or field modification can create compliance problems. Sometimes the better fix is replacing the existing device with a matching grade of hardware rather than forcing a new setup onto a weak or damaged door.

What should we do if several exterior doors are giving us trouble, but the problems seem different at each opening?

Treat each opening as its own system. A door can fail because of the lock, but also because of hinges, frame movement, bad alignment, worn closers, damaged latches, or an access control issue. Swapping cylinders alone may not fix repeated lockouts or doors that do not latch cleanly. It helps to identify which doors are life-safety exits, which need keyed access, and which are employee-only so repairs are prioritized in a sensible order.
