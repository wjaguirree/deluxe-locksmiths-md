Installed Right, Tested Right

A proper commercial lock installation starts with the door and frame, not just the lock body. We check alignment, existing holes, strike placement, hinge wear, and the way the door closes under normal use. In a place like Townsend, where some buildings sit on older grids near Main Street and others back onto open ground off Route 71, doors can settle, shift, or get patched over the years. If we force new hardware onto a bad fit, it may work for a day and then bind, drag, or leave the latch only half-seated. That's the kind of bodge that shows up later as repeated service calls, broken keys, or a lock that never feels solid.

What you should look for after the job is simple: the key should turn cleanly, the latch should engage without slamming the door, and the deadbolt should extend fully into the strike without scraping. The trim should sit flush, the screws should be anchored in the right material, and the door should open and close without rubbing at the top or along the edge. On storefronts, offices, and back-of-house doors, we also make sure the hardware matches how the door is actually used, including frequent traffic, heavier use, or the need to secure storage areas and equipment out by the outbuildings.

If the lock looks crooked, the strike plate is chewed up, or the door only locks when you pull it hard, that's not a good install. We aim for hardware that works smoothly on the first pass and keeps working after the door gets used the way real doors do.

Commercial Lock Installation in Townsend - common questions

How do you decide whether a business door in Townsend needs repair, rekeying, or a new lock?

We look at the condition of the lock, the door, and the frame. If the hardware is sound and the issue is a worn part, repair may be enough. If the lock works but the keys need to change, rekeying is often the right move. If the lock is damaged, outdated, or not suited to the door, replacement is the better choice.

What should we have ready before you install commercial locks at our shop or office?

It helps to know which doors need attention, who should have access, and whether the lock is for an entry, a back door, or a storage area. If there are existing keys, bring them along. If the door has been sticking or the frame has damage, that information helps us plan the work and avoid surprises once we're on site.

Can a worn storefront lock be rekeyed, or does it usually need replacement?

It depends on the condition of the hardware. Rekeying only changes the key pattern inside a working lock. If the lock is loose, damaged, missing parts, or no longer latches cleanly, rekeying won't fix that. In those cases, replacing the lock makes more sense and gives the door a better result.

We lost track of who has keys to a storage room. Is rekeying enough?

Often, yes, if the lock is still in good shape. Rekeying lets us change access without changing the whole lock. If you also want a stronger grade of hardware for a storage room, or the current lock is already worn, we may recommend replacement instead. That gives you better control going forward.

What if the door is older and the lock type isn't common anymore?

That happens on older properties around Townsend. We can usually work with standard commercial hardware, but some doors need adaptation or a different lock style to fit properly. If the opening has been repaired before or the frame is out of square, we'll look at the whole setup and choose hardware that matches the door instead of forcing a bad fit.
