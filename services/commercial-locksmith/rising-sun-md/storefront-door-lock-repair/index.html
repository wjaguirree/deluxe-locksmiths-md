Repair, Rekey, or Replace

When a storefront door starts giving trouble, we look at what is actually worn or damaged before we suggest anything else. A sticky mortise cylinder, a bent hook bolt, or a threshold that's out of line can often be repaired and put back into service without changing the whole setup. If the problem is a lost key, a former employee, or a key that still turns but shouldn't, rekeying may be the cleaner answer. If the hardware is cracked, stripped, corroded, or no longer matches the door and frame, replacement makes more sense than trying to nurse it along.

That judgment matters in a place like downtown Rising Sun, where a shop door may see steady daily use and the weather can work on aluminum hardware over time. We check how the door hangs, how the pivots move, whether the latch is meeting the strike correctly, and whether the cylinder is still worth keeping. If the lock body is sound, we'll repair or rekey it and keep the rest of the assembly in place. If the door has been forced, if keys are missing and the hardware is already failing, we may recommend replacing the worn parts so the door closes and secures the way it should.

Storefront Door Lock Repair in Rising Sun - common questions

Why won't my storefront door lock stay caught even though the key still turns?

That usually means the lock is only part of the problem. On aluminum storefront doors, a worn hook bolt, a loose pivot, or a shifted strike can keep the latch from holding. We check the way the door meets the frame, then repair the part that's actually causing the failure so the door closes and secures the way it should.

What should I have ready before you come look at my door in Rising Sun?

It helps to know which door is acting up, what it's doing, and whether anyone tried to force it or use a different key. If you have extra keys or the old hardware, keep those nearby. Those details help us decide whether the issue is the cylinder, the alignment, or another worn part in the door.

Is it a bad sign if my storefront door is hard to close at the end of the day?

Yes, it can be. A hard-close door often means the pivot has shifted, the threshold is worn, or the latch is rubbing where it shouldn't. That kind of wear can lead to a lock failure later. We check the whole closing action so the door isn't left depending on a part that's already out of line.

Should I repair the mortise cylinder or replace the whole storefront lock?

That depends on what's worn out. If the cylinder is the only failed part, repair or replacement of that piece may be enough. If the hook bolt, latch case, or door alignment is also off, replacing only the cylinder won't solve the problem. We look at the full setup and explain which choice fits the hardware.

Can you fix a storefront door that someone bent trying to pry it open?

Often, yes, but the lock repair may only be part of the job. Pry damage can bend the frame, throw off the pivot, or warp the strike area so the door no longer lines up. We check the door, frame, and locking hardware together, then repair what can be restored and point out anything that can't be trusted as-is.
