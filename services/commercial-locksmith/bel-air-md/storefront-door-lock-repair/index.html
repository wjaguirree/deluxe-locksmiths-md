When a storefront lock starts sticking, grinding, or not lining up with the door, we start by checking the door itself, not just the cylinder. Aluminum storefront doors can sag, the threshold can shift, and a worn pivot or loose mortise case can make a lock look bad when the real problem is the fit. We'll look at the hook bolt, latch, strike, cylinder, and closer, then decide what needs repair and what can be saved.

That matters for the law offices, medical suites, and shops around the Harford County Courthouse and Main Street, where a door has to close cleanly and secure without being fussy. If the lock can be repaired, we do that. If the hardware is worn out, we replace the right parts and adjust the door so it operates the way it should. Most of this work is done on site, so we come to the building, make the fix, and get the entry back in working order without turning it into a bigger project than it needs to be.

Aluminum storefront doors fail in a few predictable ways: the lock body loosens, the mortise cylinder starts sticking, the hook bolt doesn't throw cleanly, the pivot gets sloppy, or the threshold and strike stop lining up. On a storefront in Bel Air, the right fix usually starts with the door itself. If the door is sound and the hardware is serviceable, repair is often the cleanest path. That can mean tightening and resetting the lock, replacing worn parts, realigning the door, or swapping just the cylinder when the key problem is the issue.

Rekeying makes sense when the hardware still works but the key control needs to change. That comes up after a tenant change, staff turnover, a lost key, or a concern about who still has access. Replacing is the better call when the lock body is damaged, parts are worn past a reliable repair, or the existing hardware just won't hold adjustment. Around the courthouse and Main Street, where law offices and medical suites need doors that close, latch, and secure properly, the goal is simple: fix what can be trusted, rekey when access needs to change, and replace when the lock is no longer worth patching.

The door has to match the hardware. If the pivot is out of alignment, a new cylinder won't solve a dragging door. If the hook bolt isn't catching because the strike is off, the lock may be fine and the frame needs adjustment. The useful decision is never just "repair or replace." It's whether the door, the lock, and the access needs still fit together.

What to Check First

If your storefront door starts dragging, won't latch cleanly, or the key turns but nothing catches, it helps to gather a few details before we come out. Note whether the problem shows up on the door itself, the frame, or the threshold, and whether it's worse after weather changes or when the door is used heavily. A quick photo of the face of the door, the edge hardware, and the cylinder can save a lot of back-and-forth, especially for shops near Main Street where older aluminum doors and mixed hardware are common.

What we want you to leave alone is the part that can turn a repair into a bigger job. Don't force the door shut, don't pry at the latch or hook bolt, and don't pull apart the mortise cylinder or pivot assembly just to see what's inside. Those parts are matched closely, and a small bend or stripped screw can throw the whole door out of line. If the closer is slamming, the threshold is worn, or the door rubs the frame, those clues help us decide whether the lock needs adjustment, parts replacement, or a deeper alignment repair. Around Bel Air, we see a lot of storefronts tied to busy foot traffic and daily use, so we focus on restoring smooth operation without disturbing the pieces that still work.

Most calls start the same way: the customer says the key turns, but the door still won't latch, or the handle feels loose, or the lock used to work and now it sticks. On the phone, the important details are the door type, whether it's an aluminum storefront door, whether the key is turning at all, and whether the problem is with locking, closing, or both. That helps narrow down whether this is a hardware issue, a door alignment issue, or a cylinder that needs to be changed.

Another common question is whether the whole lock has to be changed if a key is lost or an employee leaves. Not always. If the hardware is in good shape, rekeying the cylinder may solve the access problem without changing the rest of the door hardware. If the lock is broken, the hook bolt won't engage, or the parts are worn out, replacement can be the better answer. Sometimes the real issue is the pivot or threshold, not the key side at all.

Storefront Door Lock Repair in Bel Air - common questions

My storefront door near Main Street won't latch unless I lift it. Is that a lock problem or a door problem?

That usually points to alignment more than the key side of the lock. On aluminum storefront doors, worn pivots, a sagging door, or a shifted strike can keep the hook bolt from catching. The lock may still be usable, but the door and frame need to be checked together so the latch line up properly.

If I lost keys for my Bel Air shop, do I need a new lock or can it just be rekeyed?

If the existing storefront hardware is in good shape, rekeying is often the right move. It changes which key works without replacing the full lock body. If the cylinder is damaged, the lock is worn, or the hardware is already acting up, replacement may make more sense than trying to keep an old part going.

What should I have ready when I call about a storefront lock at the courthouse side of town?

It helps to know the door type, what part is failing, and whether the issue is new or something that has been getting worse. If you can, note whether the key turns, whether the handle feels loose, and whether the door closes normally. That gives a clearer picture before anyone comes out to look at it.

Can a mortise cylinder be changed without replacing the whole aluminum door lock?

Yes, in many cases it can. If the lock body, hook bolt, and door hardware are still working well, changing the mortise cylinder is a practical way to update access. If the internal parts are worn or the hardware is damaged, the cylinder alone may not solve the problem and the full assembly may need attention.

What if the lock works but the threshold or pivot is worn too?

Then the job is bigger than a simple rekey. A working lock can still fail to secure the door if the pivot is loose or the threshold is out of line. In that case, the repair may involve adjusting the door hardware first, then servicing or replacing the lock parts that are actually worn.
