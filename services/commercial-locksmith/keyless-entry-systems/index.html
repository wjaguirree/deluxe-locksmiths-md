Keyless Entry Systems

Keypad, fob and access-control systems that end the ritual of collecting keys from departing staff. Available across Northeast Maryland & Delaware.

Call (443) 545-2775 Request service

A lot of people think keyless entry is just a keypad by the door. It isn't. For a business, it's a way to stop handing out keys, calling for them back, and wondering who still has access after a shift change. We set up keypad, fob, and access-control systems that fit the way your crew actually works, whether you need one door managed cleanly or several entry points tied together.

That matters when staff change often, managers need tighter control, or you've got people coming and going at different times. With the right system, you can add or remove access without replacing hardware every time someone leaves. We work on site across Northeast Maryland and Delaware, and most of these jobs are handled where your doors are, so there's no need to pull your operation apart just to get the system in place.

If you're replacing worn-out locks or moving away from a pile of keys, we can look at what's there now and build from that. We handle commercial keyless entry for businesses that want simpler control, cleaner handoffs, and less uncertainty at the end of the day.

When you actually need this

Keyless entry starts making sense when the real problem is control, not just wear on a lock. If you have turnover, rotating crews, outside cleaners, vendors, or managers who open and close on different days, metal keys become hard to track. One copied key can stay out in the wild long after an employee leaves. A keypad, fob reader, or small access-control setup lets you add or remove access without collecting every old key back. It also helps when one door needs different rules than another, like a front entrance that opens for staff at set times while a stockroom, office, or medication cabinet area stays limited to a smaller group. For a shop, office suite, church, warehouse, or medical office off Route 40, that change can solve a daily management headache, not just a lock problem.

You also need it when accountability matters. A mechanical key opens the door, but it usually does not tell you who used it. With credential-based entry, each user gets a code, fob, card, or phone credential tied to that person. That matters after a disputed entry, a missing item, or repeated "who locked up last night" confusion. It is also useful when a business has grown past the point where one manager knows every keyholder personally. Another common reason is repeated rekeying. If you are changing cylinders every time staffing changes, keyless can be the cleaner long-term answer because access changes happen in programming rather than at the pin tumbler. On exterior aluminum storefront doors, this often works alongside electric strikes, magnetic locks, request-to-exit devices, and panic hardware, depending on how the door is built and what the code requirements are.

But keyless entry is not always the right first move. If the issue is a sagging storefront door, a misaligned latch, a failing closer, cracked lever trim, or a panic bar that is not dogging down properly, electronics will not fix the root problem. A reader on a bad door just gives you an expensive bad door. In those cases, door closer installation, storefront door lock repair, panic bar work, or straight commercial lock repair is the better call. The same goes for a business that only wants to stop an old key from working after one employee leaves. If everyone else should keep their existing keys, commercial lock rekeying may solve it with less disruption. If the need is tiered access across several rooms but you still want physical keys, a master key system may fit better than electronic credentials.

There are also situations where a hybrid setup is the sensible answer. Many businesses still want a keyed cylinder as backup while using a keypad or fob reader for day-to-day entry. That can be the right move where power loss, weather exposure, or heavy traffic affects hardware choice. Some doors are good candidates for standalone keypad locks, while others need a full access-control panel because you want multiple doors, audit history, schedules, or safe egress tied into fire alarm requirements. The point is to match the hardware to the actual problem. If the burden is managing people and access changes, keyless entry is usually the right service. If the burden is worn hardware, bad alignment, or a simpler key hierarchy, one of the related commercial lock services is often the better answer.

How the work is done

The job starts with the door, not the keypad. A proper site visit is about figuring out how people actually use the opening and what the door can support. On a front office door, a storeroom door, and a rear employee entrance, the right hardware may be different even if the owner asks for "keyless" on all of them. The first step is checking the door material, frame condition, handing, closer, latch alignment, and existing lock prep. A hollow metal door with a narrow stile takes different hardware than an aluminum storefront door. If there's panic hardware, that affects what can be electrified and what has to stay code-compliant for egress. The next part is deciding how credentials will work in practice: keypad only, fobs or cards, or both. That choice affects the controller, reader style, audit trail, and how easy it will be to add or remove users later.

Once the opening is evaluated, the system is matched to the use. For a single interior office, a standalone keypad lever or keypad deadbolt may be enough. For employee entrances or doors that need better control, the usual setup is a reader on the secure side of the frame, an electrified lock or electric strike at the latch, a power supply, and a controller that stores users and schedules. In some buildings, a maglock is considered, but that only makes sense where the door, life-safety requirements, and traffic pattern support it. Trade-offs matter. Electric strikes can work well with an existing mechanical lever set, but they depend on good latch and frame alignment. Electrified panic bars are cleaner on some exits but cost more in parts and labor. Battery-powered standalone units avoid running cable through finished walls, but they have fewer integration options and need battery maintenance.

Installation is the part people rarely see. Existing hardware comes off, door prep is checked, and any worn parts that would cause trouble later are dealt with first. If the latch is dragging, if the closer is slamming, or if the frame is spread, electronic hardware won't fix that by itself. Low-voltage cabling may need to pass through the frame with a power transfer hinge or door loop, and the power supply has to be placed where it can be serviced without turning a simple reset into a scavenger hunt. Then the lock side and reader side are wired, terminated, tested, and protected from pinch points and loose connections. On storefront doors, the difference between a clean, reliable install and a troublesome one often comes down to door movement, backset limits, and whether the hardware was chosen to suit a narrow stile opening in the first place.

After the hardware is mounted and powered, the system is programmed around the customer's staff structure instead of around a generic template. That means setting user groups, schedules, holiday rules, and deciding who can add or delete credentials. Departed employees' fobs can be removed from the system without rekeying every cylinder, but the mechanical override still needs a plan, and that's where key control and related services like master key work can tie in. The last step is live testing at the door: valid credentials, invalid credentials, inside egress, latch relock, door held open behavior, and what happens after a power interruption. Most call-backs come from a small number of issues: weak batteries in standalone units, readers mounted where weather gets to them, doors that never latched right to begin with, and users being given permissions that don't match how the building runs. Getting those details right is what makes a keyless system useful instead of just new.

What it takes to do it properly

A proper keyless entry job starts before any hardware goes on the door. The first step is figuring out how the opening is built and how people actually use it. A narrow aluminum storefront takes different hardware than a hollow metal rear door or a wood office door. The tech needs to know door handing, backset, stile width, latch spacing, whether there's a closer fighting the latch, and whether the frame is true. On many commercial doors, the problem isn't the keypad or reader at all. It's sag, bad alignment, a worn strike, or a closer that slams the door and shakes wires loose over time. If that isn't corrected first, even good equipment ends up blamed for a door problem.

The equipment has to match the site and the level of control the customer needs. That can mean a standalone keypad lever for one office, a weather-rated keypad at a side entrance, or a full access-control setup with credential readers, electric strikes, maglocks where they are appropriate, request-to-exit devices, door position contacts, power supplies, and battery backup. Fob systems and card readers sound simple until you get into credential management, audit trails, schedules, and what happens when internet service drops or the power cuts out. A proper install also means using the right cable, protecting it through the hinge side or frame, mounting hardware square, setting closing pressure correctly, and making sure the lock still works cleanly from both sides without binding.

Information matters as much as tools. The job needs a clear list of who should have access, which doors should unlock on schedule, who needs after-hours entry, and what should happen when someone leaves. In a small office near Main Street, a manager may want one code per employee. At a warehouse off Route 40, separate credentials by shift may make more sense. Good judgment is knowing when a simple keypad is enough and when it becomes a headache because too many people share one code, no one changes it, and there's no record of who came in. It also means planning for fire egress, life-safety rules, and the fact that the cleanest wiring path is not always the safest or most serviceable one.

What separates a proper result from a bodged one is reliability a few months later, not how tidy it looked on day one. A bodged install often shows up as a crooked reader, loose trim, exposed wire, stripped mounting screws, a maglock stuck on a weak header, or a keypad added to a door that still doesn't latch every time. Another common mistake is mixing hardware that doesn't belong together, like pairing the wrong strike with a preload-prone door, or putting exterior hardware outside without checking weather exposure. Done properly, the system locks, unlocks, and relocks the way the building needs; users understand it; departing staff can be removed without chasing keys; and the next service call is for an actual change, not to fix avoidable install errors.

Getting it right the first time

Most trouble with keyless entry starts before any hardware goes on the door. A keypad or fob reader can be perfectly good and still be the wrong fit for the opening, the traffic, or the way the staff actually uses the building. A narrow aluminum storefront door takes different prep than a hollow metal back door. A panic bar with electric latch retraction behaves differently from a cylindrical lever with an electric strike. If the first visit is just "put a keypad here," the second visit usually comes from something that was missed: no protected cable path, no room in the frame for the strike body, weak door alignment, or a closer that slams hard enough to shake the latch loose. Getting it right means checking the door, frame, handing, power, and code requirements before choosing the reader and lock setup.

Another common mistake is treating every site like a single front door. A small office may do fine with a standalone keypad lock that stores codes at the lock itself. A shop with turnover, delivery access, and a rear employee entrance usually needs something more deliberate: separate credentials for different users, an audit trail, a schedule, and a way to remove access without chasing down keys from departing staff. Fobs, cards, keypad credentials, and phone-based credentials all work, but they don't all age the same. Keypads are simple, but codes get shared. Fobs are easy to issue, but they get lost. Cloud-managed systems are convenient, but only if the network and power are stable. Battery-powered locks save wiring work, but they still need a maintenance plan, especially on doors that cycle all day.

The hardware around the reader matters as much as the reader itself. A good access-control setup can still fail if the strike is underpowered, the latch is worn, the hinge side is sagging, or the closer isn't adjusted so the door actually latches every time. On older storefronts near Route 40, we often find that the lock problem is really a door condition problem wearing an electronic part out early. On pairs of doors, the inactive leaf and coordinator can create issues that look like credential problems but are really sequencing problems. That's why a proper install often includes commercial lock repair, closer adjustment, or panic bar work at the same time instead of pretending the electronics live in a vacuum.

Avoiding a second visit comes down to scope and testing. The right job includes confirming who needs access, when they need it, what happens during a power loss, how credentials will be managed, and whether the opening should fail safe or fail secure. It also means testing the door under normal use, not just with the door standing open on the bench. At a building off W Main St, that may mean checking how the latch behaves after the closer pulls the door shut, whether the request-to-exit device is placed where people naturally use it, and whether staff can understand the system without a long explanation. A clean first install is less about flashy hardware and more about matching the parts, the wiring, and the door condition so the system works day after day.

What to have ready

Before you call, it helps to pin down what you want the system to do, not just what door it's going on. A back employee entrance has different needs than a front glass storefront or an interior office door. Think about who needs access, whether they'll use a code, a key fob, a card, or a phone credential, and whether certain people should only get in during certain hours. If you already have hardware in place, check the door type and what's mounted on it now: cylindrical lever lock, mortise lock, panic bar, electric strike, maglock, door closer, or an existing keypad that's become unreliable. If a code pad works sometimes and fails other times, note what it's doing. Common issues include worn buttons, a loose reader, intermittent power, bad relays, misaligned latches, and doors that don't pull fully shut, which can make a good access system look like a bad one.

It also helps to gather the basic facts about the opening itself. A narrow aluminum storefront door often takes a different approach than a hollow metal rear door or a wood office door. If you can, have photos ready of both sides of the door, the frame, the hinge side, the strike area, and any closer or panic hardware. If there's an existing panel, transformer, card reader, request-to-exit device, or electric strike, include that too. Let us know whether the system needs to stand alone at one door or tie together multiple doors across the site. If internet access, remote management, audit trails, or integration with a buzzer, intercom, or camera matter to you, say that upfront. On older buildings near Main Street in Elkton, door condition and frame condition can matter as much as the electronics.

On the day of service, the first step is usually confirming the door's condition and the handing, clearance, power source, and life-safety hardware before any recommendation is finalized. If someone on your side can answer who should have access and who should not, the job goes much smoother. For businesses that have had staff turnover, it's smart to know whether you want to delete old credentials only, replace hardware entirely, or move to a system with user logs and scheduled access. If you have a current code list, fob count, or record of which former employees still have credentials, have that ready. If the issue is a failure rather than an upgrade, describe exactly what changed: dead keypad, reader beeping but not unlocking, door unlocking but not latching, or strike buzzing with no release.

Once on site, the work is typically about matching the system to the real door and how your staff uses it, then setting it up so it closes, latches, and grants access consistently. That may include replacing worn mechanical hardware so the electronic side isn't fighting a sagging door, a bad closer, or a damaged latch. After installation or repair, you can expect a walkthrough of how users are added and removed, how codes or fobs are managed, what the lock does during a power loss if backup power is involved, and what routine upkeep matters most. On multi-tenant properties or busier commercial sites off Route 40, clear authority from the owner, manager, or tenant contact helps avoid delays and keeps the work focused on the right doors.

Frequently asked questions

Can a keyless entry system be set up so different employees only have access to certain doors or certain times of day?

Yes. Most commercial systems can be programmed by user, door, and schedule. That lets you give office staff weekday access, limit storage or back-room access, and shut off entry outside normal business hours. It also makes turnover easier because you can remove one code, fob, or credential without changing hardware for everyone else. The right setup depends on how many doors you need to control and how your staff actually moves through the building.

What happens if the power goes out or the internet drops? Do the doors stop working?

Usually, no. A well-planned system should keep the lock hardware operating even if your internet connection fails, because door control is normally handled locally. Battery-backed components can help during a power outage. The bigger question is how you want the doors to behave during a failure: stay locked, unlock for safe exit, or allow only certain credentials. That needs to be decided up front so the system matches your building's safety needs and daily use.

Can you use keypads, fobs, and phone credentials together, or do you have to pick one method?

You can often mix them, and that's common in commercial buildings. A front door might use phone credentials for managers, fobs for employees, and a keypad code for a cleaning crew or vendor. The important part is keeping permissions organized so you don't create workarounds that weaken security. In some cases, combining too many credential types makes administration messy, so it's better to match each door to the people who use it most.

Is it a good idea to put keyless entry on every door, including older exterior doors?

Not always. Some doors are good candidates for electronic hardware, and some are better left mechanical or upgraded first. Older doors, weak frames, or badly aligned latches can cause ongoing trouble no matter how smart the access system is. In a lot of buildings, the best approach is selective: secure the main entry, employee entrance, and sensitive interior doors first, then decide whether the rest of the building benefits from added control.
