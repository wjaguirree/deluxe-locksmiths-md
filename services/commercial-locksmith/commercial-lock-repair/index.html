Commercial Lock Repair

High-traffic door hardware repaired so a door that opens hundreds of times a day keeps working. Available across Northeast Maryland & Delaware.

A sticking commercial lock isn't always a sign that the whole door has to be replaced. A lot of the time, the problem is wear in the lock body, a bent latch, loose hardware, or a door that's started to shift from heavy daily use. We repair those problems for businesses across Cecil County and nearby areas so the door keeps doing its job without turning into a bigger repair. If the key is hard to turn, the latch won't catch cleanly, or the lock feels rough, we can sort out what's worn and what still has life left in it.

Because most of our work is done on site, we show up ready to deal with the real condition of the door, not a shop sample. That matters on stores, offices, warehouses, and other high-traffic entrances that open all day and take a beating from customers, employees, and deliveries. We repair and adjust commercial locks, cylinders, latch parts, strike plates, and related hardware when that's the right fix. If the hardware is beyond repair, we can replace it with something that fits the door and the way it's used. From our base in Elkton, we serve Northeast Maryland and Delaware with the kind of repair work that keeps a busy door working the way it should.

When you actually need this

Commercial lock repair makes sense when the door hardware is still the right hardware for the opening, but wear, impact, weather, or heavy daily use has knocked it out of proper working order. That usually shows up as a storefront mortise lock that binds before it fully throws, a lever set that droops and doesn't return level, a latch that won't catch unless the door is pulled hard, or a cylinder that turns rough even with the correct key. On busy entries, the problem often isn't just the lock body. A loose closer, sagging hinge, worn strike, or shifted aluminum frame can put the latch and strike out of line, so staff start forcing the key or slamming the door to get it to secure. If a door opens over and over through the day, that kind of strain spreads fast from one part of the opening to the next.

You usually need this service after the first clear signs of failure, not after the lock has completely quit. A deadlatch that sometimes stays retracted, panic hardware that doesn't relatch consistently, or a narrow stile storefront door that has developed play at the cylinder are all repair calls. So is a key that has become harder to turn at closing time, since that can point to pin wear, tailpiece problems, a loose cam, or a mortise cylinder backing out of position. In an office, retail space, church, warehouse, or small medical suite, these are more than annoyances. They lead to doors being left unsecured, staff using the wrong amount of force, and premature damage to hardware that might otherwise have been kept in service.

There are also times when repair is not the best answer. If keys have been lost, copied beyond your control, or you've had employee turnover, rekeying is usually the right move because the lock may work fine mechanically. If the existing hardware is the wrong grade for the traffic, replacing it with a better commercial lever set, mortise lock, or exit device is smarter than repeatedly fixing light-duty parts. If you're trying to control who enters certain rooms, a master key system or keyless entry setup solves a different problem than repair. And if the issue is mainly a failing closer, bent panic bar, or damaged glass storefront hardware, that may call for door closer service, panic hardware work, or storefront door lock repair rather than a standard lock repair visit.

A good repair call is about sorting out the whole opening and deciding what can be restored to dependable operation. In older buildings around Elkton, and on high-traffic entries near U.S. 40, that often means tightening trim, correcting cylinder fit, replacing worn internal parts, adjusting strikes, and checking how the lock works with the door, frame, and closer together. The goal is not to patch over a symptom. It's to get a commercial door locking, latching, and keying the way it should so daily traffic doesn't keep turning a small problem into a replacement job.

How the work is done

Most commercial lock repair starts with the whole opening, not just the key cylinder. A storefront door, office entry, or rear service door can have problems that look like a bad lock but really come from sagging hinges, a worn closer, a bent latch, or a strike that no longer lines up with the frame. The first step is to check how the door moves through its full swing, how the latch meets the strike, whether the key turns smoothly from both sides, and whether the inside trim, lever, or thumbturn has excess play. On aluminum storefront doors, the technician also checks the narrow stile for looseness around the mortise cutout and looks for signs that the hardware has shifted from repeated use. On hollow metal doors, the condition of the edge prep and mounting screws matters just as much as the lock body itself.

Once the symptoms are clear, the hardware is taken apart far enough to inspect the working parts without turning the job into unnecessary replacement. Depending on the door, that may mean servicing a cylindrical lock, a mortise lock, a deadlatch, an exit device trim, or an interconnected lockset. Typical failures include worn latch springs, broken tailpieces, set screws that have backed out, lever return springs that no longer bring the handle back to level, and cylinders that are sticking because of debris, wear, or the wrong lubricant. A proper repair may involve re-securing the chassis, replacing failed internal parts, correcting handing, tightening through-bolts, cleaning out the cylinder, and making sure the cam or tailpiece is the correct length for the lock body. If a key has become hard to use, the issue may be key wear, pin wear inside the cylinder, or a cylinder that has shifted slightly so the cam is binding under load.

A big part of the work is correcting alignment so the repaired lock is not forced every time the door closes. High-traffic doors often fail because the latch is scraping the strike, the closer is slamming the door, or the weatherstrip is putting side pressure on the latchbolt. The repair can include adjusting the strike position, shimming or resetting hardware, replacing machine screws with the proper length and thread, and addressing frame or closer issues that are shortening the life of the lock. On panic hardware, the dogging mechanism, trim spindle, and latch retraction need to work together cleanly. On a glass-and-aluminum entrance near a busy corridor off Route 40, even a small misalignment can show up fast when the door cycles all day.

Before the job is finished, the lock is tested under real operating conditions: key operation from the exterior, egress from the interior, latch engagement with the door closed, and repeated openings to confirm the fix holds under normal use. If the opening is part of a master key system, the cylinder is checked so any repaired or replaced parts still match the intended keying plan. The goal is not just to make the lock turn again, but to restore dependable function across the whole door assembly. In older buildings around Main Street, that sometimes means balancing repair against the limits of worn doors and frames, then recommending replacement only when the hardware is too far gone or the opening is no longer safe to rely on.

What it takes to do it properly

A proper commercial lock repair starts with identifying exactly what is failing, because the lock is often only one part of the problem. On a busy aluminum storefront, the complaint might be "the key sticks" when the real cause is a sagging door, a worn pivot, a misaligned strike, or a deadlatch that is no longer meeting the frame correctly. On a hollow metal door, a mortise lock may have internal wear in the hub or latch retractor, but the door closer could also be slamming the door hard enough to beat up the latch and cylinder over time. Good repair work means arriving with the tools and parts to inspect the full opening: cylinder gauges, calipers, pinning supplies, mortise cylinders, rim cylinders, Adams Rite style components, cam options, tailpieces, strikes, through-bolts, fasteners, lubricant meant for locks rather than hinges, and the hand tools to remove hardware without chewing up the door or frame.

The information matters just as much as the equipment. Before any repair, you need to know the door type, handing, backset, latch function, cylinder type, and how the space is used through the day. A narrow stile glass door at a shop near Main Street has different demands than a steel service door at a warehouse. If the opening is tied into a master key system, swapping parts without checking keyway compatibility can create a bigger problem than the original failure. If there is panic hardware, the dogging function, latch projection, and strike alignment all need to be checked together. If the lock is part of a keyless entry setup, the issue may involve the mechanical chassis, power transfer, request-to-exit hardware, or the way the lever returns under use. Guesswork is what leads to repeat failures.

Judgement is what separates a proper result from a bodged one. Some hardware should be rebuilt because the door prep is sound and the rest of the opening is worth saving. In other cases, repair is the wrong call because the lock body is cracked, the mounting is wallowed out, the trim is loose, or the frame has shifted enough that fresh parts will still bind. A proper commercial locksmith also knows when not to force a mismatched cylinder, when a longer cam will cause drag, when threadlocker belongs on a screw and when it will make future service harder, and when an electric strike problem is really a frame or alignment problem. In Harford County, that kind of judgement matters on doors that may cycle all day under steady use.

The difference shows up after the tools are packed away. A sound repair leaves the key turning cleanly, the latch or bolt moving fully, the lever returning correctly, and the door closing and securing without being babied by staff. The cylinder should sit straight, the faceplate should fit flush, the strike should take the latch without grinding, and the fasteners should be correct for the hardware instead of improvised from a random bin. A bodged job might "work" for the moment with oversized screws, a bent strike, a dry lock packed with the wrong spray, or a closer cranked up to hide alignment trouble. Proper work deals with the cause, not just the symptom, so a door that sees constant traffic can keep doing its job without becoming the next failure point.

Getting it right the first time

A commercial lock repair goes sideways when the problem gets reduced to "the lock is bad" and nobody looks at the rest of the opening. On a busy office, shop, school, or warehouse door, the lock, lever trim, latch, strike, hinges, closer, and frame all affect each other. A mortise lock may be blamed for a door that won't latch, when the real cause is a sagging leaf pulling the latch below the strike. A cylindrical lock can feel rough because the closer is slamming the door and beating up the latchbolt. Panic hardware may seem unreliable when the dogging mechanism is fine but the vertical rods are out of adjustment or the top latch is hitting a warped header. If you change one part without checking door alignment, backset, handing, strike location, and latch projection, you can leave with a new problem that shows up as soon as traffic picks back up.

Another common mistake is installing whatever is on the truck instead of matching the hardware to the door, prep, and use. Storefront aluminum doors often take narrow stile hardware with very little room for error. Hollow metal doors and wood doors have different wear patterns and different repair options. Grade matters, but so does function: classroom, storeroom, office, vestibule, and exit doors are not all supposed to behave the same way. Mixing brands without checking spindle length, cam timing, screw pattern, faceplate size, or strike geometry is how you end up with a lever that binds, a latch that barely catches, or a cylinder that turns but does not throw the bolt cleanly. The same goes for keyways and master key systems. Swapping a cylinder without checking pinning plan, tailpiece fit, and cam compatibility can create a door that works in isolation but causes trouble across the building.

The way to avoid a second visit is to treat the repair like a door system repair, not a parts swap. Wear points need to be identified before anything is replaced: ovalized mounting holes, loose through-bolts, cracked trim, worn latch hubs, mushroomed strikes, bent exit device rods, weak return springs, and closers that no longer control the sweep and latch. On a pair of doors, both leaves matter. On an exterior opening, weather, threshold condition, and frame movement matter too. A proper repair includes cycling the door repeatedly, checking key operation from the outside, checking egress from the inside, and confirming the latch seats fully without having to force the door. On a busy route near US 40, that kind of testing matters because the hardware is going right back into heavy use.

Good commercial lock repair also means knowing when not to force an old part to keep going. Some failures can be corrected with adjustment, fastening, re-tapping, or replacing the worn component only. Others call for a new mortise case, a new rim device, fresh lever trim, or a corrected strike prep because the original setup has too much play to hold up. The goal is a repair that matches the door's actual traffic and abuse, not a temporary fit that only works while everything is perfectly lined up. Whether the opening is in Elkton or elsewhere in the service area, getting it right the first time comes from measuring, matching, and testing the whole assembly so the repaired door keeps latching, unlocking, and closing the way a commercial door is supposed to.

What to have ready

Before you call, it helps to look at the door the way a service tech will. Note whether it's a narrow aluminum storefront door, a hollow metal back door, or a wood office entry, and whether the problem is in the lock itself or in the way the whole opening is working together. A mortise lock, cylindrical lever lock, rim exit device, electric strike, and door closer all fail in different ways. If the key turns but the latch won't pull back, say that. If the lever feels loose, the deadlatch sticks, the panic bar drags, the closer slams, or the door has to be pushed hard to latch, say that too. Those details matter because a lock can look worn out when the real problem is a sagging door, a bad closer arm, misaligned strike, or a frame that has shifted from heavy use.

Have a few basics in front of you: the business name, exact address, the best contact number for the person on site, and whether the door is an employee entrance, customer entry, interior office, or stockroom door. If you can, check the faceplate or trim for a brand name such as Adams Rite, Von Duprin, Schlage, Falcon, or LCN. Photos help, especially one of the full door, one of the edge of the door, and one of the lock or panic hardware. It also helps to know whether the key still works, whether the door can be secured at all, and whether anyone has already tightened screws, changed a cylinder, or taped the latch to keep the door in service. If the problem is at a glass storefront on Route 40, mention whether there is single-door or pair-door hardware, because pair doors often develop alignment issues that affect the active leaf and the flush bolts together.

On the day, clear access around the opening as much as you can. Move floor mats, delivery carts, freestanding signs, and anything stacked behind the swing path. If there is an alarm tied to the opening, make sure the person who can manage that system is reachable. The same goes for anyone who controls a keypad code, access credential, or master key record. If the hardware has been acting up only at certain times, tell the tech when it usually shows up. A door that binds after the sun hits the frame, or a closer that leaks and then loses control, points in a different direction than a broken return spring in the lever.

Once on site, the work usually starts with a full check of the door, frame, hinges, latch, strike, closer, and any connected access hardware, not just the cylinder. That matters because replacing parts without correcting alignment can leave you with the same failure again. You may be told that the lock can be repaired with new springs, set screws, dogging parts, or a cylinder change, or that the smarter fix is replacement because the body is cracked, the spindle is worn, or the trim is no longer holding adjustment. In a busy retail entry near Main Street, the trade-off is often between keeping the same hardware family for fit and keying, or changing to a heavier-duty setup that better matches the traffic on that door. Either way, expect a practical explanation of what failed, what can be saved, and what needs to be brought back into line so the door closes, latches, and secures the way it should.

Frequently asked questions

Can you repair the lock if the door is sagging or rubbing, or does that need a different service?

Sometimes the lock is only part of the problem. On a busy commercial opening, a sagging door, worn hinges, a bad closer, or a shifted frame can throw the latch out of alignment and make the lock seem faulty. We check how the whole opening is working before replacing parts. If the hardware can be adjusted and repaired, that usually makes more sense than swapping the lock alone.

When is repair the right choice, and when should a commercial lock be replaced instead?

Repair makes sense when the body of the lock is still sound and the problem is wear, dirt, misalignment, loose trim, or a failed small part. Replacement is usually the better move when the lock case is cracked, the hardware has been forced, parts are no longer supported, or the wrong grade of lock was installed for the traffic on that door. A repair should leave the opening dependable, not just temporarily usable.

Can you keep our existing key system, or will a lock repair change what keys work?

In many cases, the existing key system can stay the same. If the repair is limited to alignment, latch work, trim, cylinders, or internal service, the lock can often be returned to service without changing key access. If a cylinder is damaged beyond repair, it may need to be rekeyed or replaced to match your current setup. The goal is to restore function without creating confusion for staff or management.

What usually causes a commercial lock to fail on a door that gets heavy daily use?

High-use openings wear down in predictable ways. Latches get rounded off, return springs weaken, screws loosen, cylinders gum up, and panic or lever hardware starts binding. A door closer that shuts too hard can also beat up the lock over time. In some cases, the hardware itself is fine but the strike is out of position, so staff have to pull or push the door to make it lock. That extra force speeds up failure.
