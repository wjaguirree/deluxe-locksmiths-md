Commercial Lock Rekeying

Rekey after staff turnover or a lost master, without replacing every lock in the building. Available across Northeast Maryland & Delaware.

Call (443) 545-2775 Request service

When we show up at your property, the first thing we do is check which locks need to be changed and whether they're all keyed alike, keyed differently, or part of a master system. Then we pull each cylinder, reset the pins to a new key, test the lock on the door, and make sure the old key no longer works. If there's a master key involved, we sort that out carefully so the new setup still gives the right people access without leaving the old key in play.

This is a common fix after staff turnover, a misplaced master, or a change in who should be able to get into certain rooms, closets, or entrances. It lets you keep the hardware that's already installed instead of replacing every lock in the building. We do this kind of work on site across Cecil County, Harford County, and Kent County, so the locksmith comes to the building, not the other way around. If your business is in Elkton or anywhere nearby, we can rekey the locks you use every day and get things back under control without turning the whole place into a bigger project than it needs to be.

When you actually need this

Commercial rekeying makes sense when control of the keys has changed, but the lock hardware itself is still worth keeping. The most common trigger is staff turnover: an employee leaves with keys, a contractor never returns a copy, or a master key goes missing and nobody can say where it ended up. It also fits after a tenant change, after you hand keys out during a renovation, or after a vendor had broad access for a period of time and now doesn't need it. In those cases, rekeying lets you keep the existing locksets, cylinders, and door prep while changing which keys operate them. For many offices, retail spaces, churches, medical suites, and small warehouses around Elkton, that's the practical move when the doors and closers are working well and the concern is simply who may still have a key.

It's also the right call when your key system has gotten sloppy over time. A business may start with one front door key, then add a back door cylinder, a side entrance lever, a storeroom deadbolt, and eventually a patchwork of copies that don't match any plan. Rekeying can bring those openings back under a clean key schedule, whether that means one change key per door, one key for a manager, or rebuilding the cylinders into a proper master key system. This comes up a lot after ownership changes or after repeated lock swaps by different vendors. If the hardware is solid, the cylinders can often be re-pinned and documented so the building is easier to manage going forward.

Rekeying is not the best answer when the problem is mechanical failure. If the key sticks, the plug binds, the latch doesn't retract cleanly, the door has to be pushed or lifted to lock, or the closer is slamming and knocking the lock out of alignment, the issue may be wear, damage, or poor fit rather than key control. A worn mortise cylinder, a loose Adams Rite storefront setup, a sagging aluminum door, or a panic bar with dogging problems calls for repair or replacement work first. If the hardware is the wrong grade for the building, if the keyway is badly restricted by age, or if you've had repeated break-ins at a rear door off Pulaski Highway, new hardware may be the smarter path.

Sometimes the better answer is a different service entirely. If your business has outgrown physical keys, keyless entry may solve the real problem by letting you add and remove users without collecting keys back. If one door has been forced, storefront door lock repair may be needed before any rekeying matters. If you're adding exit devices, replacing closers, or changing from cylindrical locks to mortise hardware, it usually makes sense to handle the hardware work and the rekeying together so the cylinders, keying plan, and door function all match. Rekeying is for restoring key control. It works best when the door is sound, the hardware is serviceable, and the main risk is that old keys are still out in the world.

How the work is done

Most commercial rekey jobs start with a walk-through of the doors that actually control access, not just the front entrance. That usually means checking exterior doors, employee entrances, office doors, stock rooms, and any interior doors tied into a master key system. The locksmith identifies the hardware on each opening because the process is different for a cylindrical lever lock, a mortise cylinder in an aluminum storefront door, a rim cylinder paired with a panic bar, or a small-format interchangeable core used across multiple suites. At this stage, the important question is whether the goal is to stop old keys from working at selected doors, rebuild a whole system after staff turnover, or recover control after a missing master key. The condition of the doors matters too. A rekey can be done on a lock that still operates well, but if the cylinder is worn, the tailpiece is damaged, or the latch is already failing to retract cleanly, that needs to be addressed as part of the same visit.

Once the scope is clear, the cylinder or core is removed and decoded so the pinning can be changed to a new key combination. On standard pin tumbler hardware, that means repinning the plug with the correct bottom pins and matching springs, then testing the key operation and shear line before the cylinder goes back in the door. On interchangeable core systems, the core may be repinned on site or replaced with a newly pinned core that matches the planned hierarchy. If the property uses a master key system, the work has to be mapped carefully so individual change keys still operate their assigned doors while the new master works only where intended. That is where a lot of problems show up after sloppy prior work: cross-keying, duplicated cuts that shouldn't overlap, chambers pinned inconsistently, or cylinders from different keyways mixed into one building.

After repinning, the locksmith tests the hardware as a door assembly, not just as a loose cylinder in hand. A key can turn fine on the bench and still bind once the cylinder is reinstalled in a misaligned door. On a storefront near Main Street, for example, a mortise cylinder may be functioning properly while the actual complaint comes from a sagging door, a worn cam, or an Adams Rite mechanism that needs adjustment. The same applies to panic hardware: if the rim cylinder is rekeyed but the dogging function is worn or the device isn't latching consistently, the access problem is only partly solved. Good rekey work includes checking key insertion, full rotation, latch action, and whether the door relocks correctly every time. If one door is sticking because of frame movement or a closer that is pulling the leaf out of alignment, that gets noted so the client is not left thinking a key issue caused a door problem.

The last part is control and documentation. New keys are cut, marked to match the agreed system, and tested in the locks they are supposed to operate. Any old keys that should no longer work are verified as inactive. In a larger property in Cecil County, that may also mean updating a key schedule so management knows which key opens which office, rear entrance, or storage area. Rekeying is often chosen because it preserves existing hardware that is still serviceable, but there are trade-offs. If the building has badly worn cylinders, unsupported keyways, or too many doors patched together from mismatched brands over the years, replacement may be the cleaner long-term fix on selected openings. The point of the service is controlled access with as little disruption to the building as possible, while leaving the locks operating correctly under everyday use.

What it takes to do it properly

A proper commercial rekey starts well before any pins come out of a cylinder. The locksmith needs to identify exactly what hardware is on the door and what condition it's in. That means knowing the difference between a mortise cylinder, rim cylinder, interchangeable core, small-format IC, large-format IC, and the common key-in-knob and lever cylinders found on office suites, warehouses, schools, and retail doors. It also means checking whether the lock is actually worth rekeying as-is. If the plug is badly worn, the tailpiece is loose, the cam is wrong for the cylinder, or the keyway has been chewed up by copied keys, simply changing the combination won't fix the underlying problem. A bodged job starts when someone treats every lock like the same five-pin house lock and ignores wear, hand of door, and door function.

The work itself takes the right kit and the discipline to use it correctly. For a clean result, you need the proper followers, pinning trays, depth and spacing references, factory-spec pins, springs, clips, tailpieces, shims where appropriate, lubricant suited to lock cylinders, and test keys cut to the system being used. On commercial jobs, key control matters just as much as the pinning. If a building has a master key system, the locksmith has to map every change key, master, and sub-master relationship so one opening doesn't accidentally operate another. A careless rekey can leave cross-keying, trapped chambers, or bitting conflicts that don't show up until staff starts carrying keys and finding out a side door opens a room it shouldn't.

Good judgement is what separates routine cylinder work from a result that holds up in daily use. After staff turnover or a lost master key, the question is not only whether the old keys stop working, but whether the new setup still matches how the building operates. Some sites need one key per tenant and a restricted master above that. Others are better served by splitting exterior and interior access, or by converting selected doors to interchangeable cores so future changes are cleaner. On aluminum storefront doors, the cylinder may be only one part of the issue; if the latch is dragging, the Adams Rite hardware is worn, or the closer is slamming the door out of alignment, the rekey should be paired with adjustment or repair. Otherwise the customer gets new keys but the same call-backs.

A proper finish includes testing every opening under real door conditions, not just with the cylinder in hand. The key should insert smoothly, turn without grinding, and withdraw at the correct positions. The latch or deadbolt should throw fully, the panic device should still dog or release as intended, and any master key should work only where the schedule says it should. The locksmith should also verify that duplicate cylinders on paired doors are pinned to plan, that key stamps and records make sense, and that old hardware from a building in Elkton or off Pulaski Highway hasn't been mixed into the wrong system. That's the difference between a rekey that restores control and one that only looks finished when the toolbox is closed.

Getting it right the first time

Commercial rekeying sounds simple until a building has more than one lock format in play. A front aluminum storefront door may have an Adams Rite mortise cylinder, the office doors may use standard Schlage or Kwikset key-in-knob cylinders, and the rear service entrance may have a rim cylinder tied to a panic bar. One common mistake is assuming every opening can be put on one key without checking what hardware is actually installed and what keyway it accepts. Another is rekeying only the doors everyone remembers and missing the side entrance, interior file room, gate cylinder, or a cabinet core that still takes the old staff key. Getting it right starts with a full count of cylinders, keyways, and functions before any pins are changed.

The problems that cause callbacks are usually mechanical, not just about the key cuts. A cylinder can be rekeyed correctly and still give trouble if the tailpiece is the wrong length, the cam is worn, the mortise housing is loose, or the lever return is dragging so the latch does not retract cleanly. On older commercial doors, misalignment is a big one. If the latch is rubbing the strike, people blame the new key when the real issue is door sag, a bent strike, or a closer that is pulling the door out of position. Rekey work also goes wrong when master key plans are rushed. If a building needs management access, tenant separation, and a maintenance key, the pinning has to be laid out on paper first so the hierarchy works across every cylinder instead of becoming a patchwork.

Avoiding a second visit means checking operation at the door, not only at the bench. Each rekeyed cylinder should be tested installed, under normal door pressure, with the correct key and with any master key that is supposed to operate it. On panic hardware, that means confirming the cylinder turns the trim the way it should and that dogging or latch control still functions as designed. On storefront hardware, it means verifying the key turns smoothly from both sides and that the lock fully secures when the door closes. If interchangeable core hardware is present, the core itself, the control key, and the housing all need to match. A worn housing can make a good core feel bad.

The cleanest jobs usually come from slowing down long enough to ask the right questions before repinning begins. Was there staff turnover, a lost master, a vendor key that should no longer work, or a new suite layout that changed who needs access? Does the customer want to keep the existing keys for some doors and change others, or fold the whole site into a master key system? In mixed commercial properties around Elkton and along Route 40, those details matter because one overlooked stockroom or one mismatched cylinder can leave a real security gap. Good commercial rekeying is not just changing pins. It is making sure every opening that matters is accounted for, every key level is intentional, and every lock still works the way the door was built to work.

What to have ready

Before you call, it helps to know exactly which doors and cylinders are part of the change. In a lot of buildings, the front entry, rear employee door, office doors, storage rooms, and a gate or mailbox may all be on different keyways even when the keys look similar at a glance. Gather every key you still have for the property, including master keys, sub-master keys, stamped copies, and any keys kept in a drawer for vendors or cleaning staff. If one key was lost or not returned after turnover, say that clearly, because that changes the scope. Also check whether you want the locks rekeyed to match one existing key, split into separate groups, or built into a new master key system. If there are doors that already stick, sag, or need to be pulled hard to lock, mention that too. Rekeying changes the inside code of the cylinder, but it does not fix a door that is out of alignment, a latch that drags, or a closer that slams shut and shakes the hardware loose over time.

A quick walk-through before the visit saves confusion. Look at the face of each lock and note what type it is: mortise cylinder, rim cylinder, interchangeable core, standard knob or lever cylinder, deadbolt, storefront narrow-stile hardware, or a cabinet cam lock. If you can read a brand on the cylinder or trim, have that ready. Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Arrow, Corbin Russwin, Sargent, Best, and Adams Rite all show up in commercial spaces, and the parts and key blanks are not interchangeable just because the door is commercial. If a door has panic hardware, electric strike wiring, a keyless lever, or a door closer that has been leaking oil, say so up front. Those details matter because sometimes the right answer is to rekey one cylinder and repair or replace another piece at the same visit, especially on older storefront doors along Route 40 where the lock body may have wear beyond the cylinder itself.

On the day, clear access to the doors being worked on and have someone available who can approve the keying plan. That person should know which employees need access to what, whether a landlord or property manager must keep a key, and whether any doors should stay on a separate change key for liability reasons. In a medical office, school, church, shop, or warehouse, there is usually at least one opening that cannot be left unsecured during business hours, so the work is often done in a sequence that keeps the building usable while cylinders are serviced and tested. Expect the locksmith to inspect each opening, confirm the hardware type, verify how many usable keys are in circulation, and check whether worn cylinders, loose tailpieces, bent cams, stripped set screws, or mismatched keyways will affect the plan.

Once the work is done, every rekeyed lock should be operated with the new keys from both sides where that applies, and the final key schedule should make sense to the people using it. In a small office in Elkton, that may be as simple as one key for all exterior doors and another for a records room. In a larger building, it may mean a master with separate change keys for managers, maintenance, and tenant spaces. If any old keys still turn after rekeying, that needs to be caught before the job is closed out. It is also a good time to label keys in a way that helps your staff without advertising where they belong if a ring goes missing again.

Frequently asked questions

Can you rekey only certain doors and leave the rest of our system alone?

Yes. Rekeying does not have to be all or nothing. If you want to change exterior entry doors, one office suite, a storage room, or a set of doors tied to a former employee, that can usually be done without changing every cylinder in the building. The main thing is making sure the key plan still makes sense afterward, especially if you already have master keys or shared access between departments.

Will rekeying work if our doors already use a master key system?

Usually, yes, but it needs to be checked lock by lock. A master key system depends on specific pinning so different keys open different doors in a controlled way. Rekeying one cylinder the wrong way can break that structure. If the goal is to keep master access while blocking old change keys, that is often possible. If the existing setup is messy or has been altered over time, a partial rebuild of the key system may be the better move.

Are there cases where rekeying is the wrong fix and the hardware should be replaced instead?

Yes. Rekeying changes which key operates the lock, but it does not correct worn, damaged, or poorly matched hardware. If a cylinder is failing, keys stick, the door does not latch correctly, or the lock body is loose in the door, rekeying alone will not solve the underlying problem. The same goes for doors with mismatched cylinders from different systems. In those cases, replacement or repair is usually the smarter long-term choice.

What should we do if a lost master key might still be out there?

Treat that as a security issue, not just an inconvenience. A lost master can affect many doors at once, so the first step is figuring out exactly what that key opened and whether any copies may exist. From there, the safest option may be to rekey every cylinder under that master system, not just the front doors. It also helps to tighten key control afterward by limiting duplication and keeping a clear record of who has each key.
