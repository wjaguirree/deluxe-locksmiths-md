Final Checks Matter

After the hardware is set, we don't treat the job as finished until we check how the door behaves under real use. We test the latch and deadbolt for smooth throw, make sure the key turns without dragging, and verify the strike lines up cleanly with the frame. Those checks matter because a lock can be the right model and still fail if the door or prep work is off by a small amount. On storefronts near State Street or offices off DE-1, that small miss can turn into a door that sticks, binds, or won't secure the way it should.

We also confirm the lock is matched to the door material and the way the building is used. A back-of-house steel door needs different attention than a glass entry or an interior office door. We check screw bite, cylinder fit, and edge clearance so the hardware stays tight and doesn't chew up the door over time. If the door has closer pressure, weatherstrip, or heavy daily traffic, we watch how those parts affect the latch. That keeps the lock from failing early and helps you avoid repeated service calls.

Before we wrap up, we test every key we've cut or matched, check that the locking side and the opening side both operate as expected, and verify that the door can be secured and opened without guesswork. We also look for anything that could cause trouble later, like a misaligned strike, a loose trim ring, or a bolt that's rubbing the frame. Those final checks are there to make sure the installation does what it's supposed to do the first time, not just on the bench.

Commercial Lock Installation in Dover - common questions

How does a commercial lock installation usually work for a Dover storefront or office?

We look at the door, the frame, and how the space is used, then install hardware that fits that setup. A storefront door may need different treatment than a rear office or storage entry. The goal is a lock that lines up correctly, closes cleanly, and matches the level of traffic the door sees.

What should I have ready before you install new locks at my Dover business?

It helps to know which doors need work, who should have access, and whether you want all the locks keyed alike or kept separate. If there's an old lockset, bring any keys you still have. For a rental or a changing staff situation, having the door schedule clear saves back-and-forth once the work starts.

I'm worried the new lock won't fit the door we already have. Is that a real issue?

Yes, it can be. Commercial doors vary a lot in thickness, prep, and hardware type. A lock that looks right on paper still has to match the existing cutouts and the way the door swings. If the opening is damaged or the old hardware was patched together, we may need to adjust the setup so the new lock works the way it should.

Should I choose a keyed lock or a lock with more control for different employees?

That depends on how the business runs. A small office may do fine with a simple keyed setup, while a place with several staff members, storerooms, or changing access needs may need a more organized key plan. The right choice is usually the one that keeps access simple for the right people and limited for everyone else.

Can you install new locks on a door that already has damaged hardware or a bad latch?

Sometimes yes, but not always with the existing parts left in place. If the latch area is worn out, the strike is misaligned, or the door edge is damaged, the lock may need more than a simple swap. In those cases, the installation has to address the door condition too, or the new hardware won't hold up.
