Repair, Rekey, or Replace

On a busy commercial door, the right fix depends on what's actually worn out. If the latch is sticking, the closer is slamming, or the lock body is still solid but parts are binding from daily use, we usually start with repair. That keeps the existing hardware in place and gets the door back to working the way it should. If the cylinder still turns cleanly but you need new keys after a staffing change, a rekey makes more sense. We change the pins inside the lock so old keys no longer work, without changing the whole lockset. That's often the right call for storefronts near Main Street or offices in Downtown Smyrna where the door hardware is still serviceable but access needs to be reset.

Replacement comes into play when the hardware is too far gone for a repair to hold up. Bent parts, cracked housings, stripped threads, broken mortise cases, and mismatched builder-grade hardware can keep causing the same trouble. In older buildings, especially where one door has seen decades of use, a repair may be only a short-term fix if the body of the lock is worn out. In newer construction, we also look at whether the lock is the right grade for the traffic it's getting. Our job is to sort through those options on site, explain what can be saved, what should be reset, and what needs to be swapped so the door keeps closing, latching, and securing the way your business needs it to.

Commercial Lock Repair in Smyrna - common questions

How do you tell if my Smyrna storefront lock needs repair instead of just adjustment?

A good check looks at how the lock, strike, door, and frame work together. If the latch only catches when the door is pushed hard, the key feels rough, or the handle has extra play, there may be worn parts inside the lock. An adjustment can help, but repeated sticking usually means something in the hardware needs repair.

What should I have ready before you come out to repair a commercial lock?

Have the door accessible, make sure someone can point out the problem, and if possible keep the key or keys on hand. If the lock has been sticking, binding, or failing at certain times, note when it happens. That helps narrow down whether the issue is the lock body, the cylinder, the strike, or the door itself.

Can a badly worn lock on a busy business door become a security problem?

Yes. A lock that's loose, hard to latch, or not fully engaging can leave a door easier to force or easier to leave partly unsecured without noticing. On a high-use door, wear can build quietly until the lock stops doing its job. Repairing it early is better than waiting for it to fail at closing time.

Should I repair the existing hardware or replace the whole lock?

If the body of the lock is sound, repair is often the better route. That can include servicing the cylinder, fixing alignment, or replacing worn internal parts. If the lock is cracked, badly damaged, or too worn to hold adjustment, replacement makes more sense. The right choice depends on the condition of the hardware, not just the symptom.

What if my building has an older mortise lock that's still original?

Older mortise locks can often be repaired, but they need a careful hands-on look. Parts may be worn, missing, or no longer matching well with the door. In many cases, a repair can keep the original lock working and avoid unnecessary changes to the door. If the hardware is beyond repair, you'll know what has to be replaced and why.
