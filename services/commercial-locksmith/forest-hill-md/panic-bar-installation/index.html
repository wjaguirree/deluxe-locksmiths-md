What To Check First

Before we set a panic bar, it helps to look at the door itself and gather a few basics. We need to know whether the opening is steel, aluminum, or wood, whether it swings in or out, and whether there's already a closer, latch, or old exit hardware in place. A clear photo of the inside and outside of the door, plus the frame and threshold, can save a second trip for missing details. If the building has inspection notes, tenant rules, or a fire door label, have those handy too. In Forest Hill, we often see newer storefronts and office suites off MD-24 with builder-grade prep that looks ready on the surface but still needs the right device and strike alignment.

What you should leave alone is the part most people are tempted to test. Don't drill the door, remove the old hardware, or try to bend the frame to make a latch line up. Don't paint over labels, trim bolts, or add random screws to hold a loose bar in place. Those shortcuts can create an egress problem or make the door fail inspection. If the opening serves a hallway, stairwell, or other exit route, we also want to know who uses it and when the door is supposed to alarm, relock, or stay secure. That kind of information matters more than guessing at the hardware.

We handle the fit, spacing, and code details on site, but the best starting point is a clean look at the door before anyone touches it. That gives us a straightforward path to an installation that works the way it should and is ready when an inspector checks the opening.

Panic Bar Installation in Forest Hill - common questions

How does panic bar installation usually work on a storefront door in Forest Hill?

First we look at the door, frame, and current hardware to see what the opening can support. Then we choose the right exit device for the door type and use. The work usually involves fitting the bar, latch, and trim so the door opens easily from the inside and closes securely from the outside.

What should I have ready before you install a panic bar at my office or shop?

It helps to know which door is the main exit, whether the door is wood, metal, or glass, and whether there's already any hardware mounted there. If the door is part of an alarm or access control setup, have that information ready too. That saves guesswork and helps the install go smoothly.

What if the door already has old exit hardware that keeps sticking?

That's common on doors that have been patched, painted, or used hard over time. Sometimes the existing device can be repaired, but often the latch, strike, or mounting points are worn out. If the parts no longer line up well, replacing the hardware is usually the cleaner fix.

Should I choose a rim panic bar or a vertical rod device for my building?

It depends on the door and how it's used. A single swinging exit door often works well with a rim device. Taller openings, pairs of doors, or certain fire-rated setups may call for a vertical rod system instead. The right choice depends on the opening, not just what looks standard.

Can a panic bar be installed on a side door that gets used for deliveries too?

Often yes, as long as the door and frame are suitable for the device and the opening still works as a proper exit. A delivery door may need extra attention if it sees heavy use, has a closer on it, or ties into access control. The hardware has to fit the way the door is actually used.
