Panic Bar Installation in Cecilton - common questions

How do you tell whether my exit door needs a new panic bar or just an adjustment?

A loose handle, a door that doesn't latch, or a bar that feels rough doesn't always mean the whole device is bad. Sometimes the closer is pulling the door off line, the strike is misaligned, or the hinges are worn. The first step is checking how the door closes and whether the latch actually meets the frame cleanly.

What should I have ready before you install a panic bar at my Cecilton building?

It helps to know whether the door is wood, steel, or aluminum, and whether it's a single door or part of a pair. If you have building rules, landlord approval, or inspection notes, bring those along. Photos of the door from both sides can also help identify the right hardware before any work starts.

Can a panic bar be installed on an older door without creating a security problem?

Usually, yes, if the door and frame are still sound. The inside hardware should let people exit easily, while the outside side should stay secure unless it's designed differently for that opening. If the door is warped, badly patched, or the frame is loose, those issues need attention first or the new device won't perform right.

Should I replace the whole panic device or keep part of the old setup?

That depends on what failed. If the push pad works but the latch, trim, or rods are worn, part replacement may make sense. If the device is bent, the mounting holes are damaged, or the parts no longer line up, a full replacement is usually the cleaner fix. The door's condition matters just as much as the hardware.

What if my door is on a back entrance used by staff only?

It still needs to open correctly from the inside if it serves as an exit. Staff-only use doesn't remove the need for proper egress hardware. In a small business or shop outside Cecilton, back doors often see rough treatment, so the device has to match the door and hold up to daily use without sticking or binding.
