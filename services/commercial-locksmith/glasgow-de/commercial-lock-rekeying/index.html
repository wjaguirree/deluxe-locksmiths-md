When a key turns up missing or a manager hands back a set they should've kept, the worry starts right away. Who still has access, which doors are affected, and do you really need to replace every lock to get control back. For businesses around Route 896 and the plazas nearby, that kind of problem can spread fast because one key often ties into more than one door, side entrance, or storage room.

We rekey commercial locks so you can keep the hardware you already have and change who can open it. That's often the right move after staff turnover, a lost master key, or a change in who manages the building. We work on site for offices, restaurants, storefronts, and other commercial spaces, and we keep the process focused on getting your doors back under control without turning the whole place upside down. If a lock is worn out or not worth keeping, we'll talk through that too.

Commercial rekeying starts with us checking which doors, cores, and keyways are actually in use, then we compare them to the access you want to keep. That order matters because a bad rekey plan can leave a back door, storeroom, or office with the wrong key control even if the main entrance is handled right. On a lot of jobs around the Route 896 and US-40 junction, we're working in parking lots, loading areas, or side entries first so staff can keep the business moving while we sort the locks one by one.

After that, we rekey the cylinders, build the new keying pattern, and test each lock before we hand anything back. Testing comes last because it shows whether the new key works smoothly and whether an old key still opens something it shouldn't. If you have a master key system, we handle the higher-level access first so the rest of the change makes sense. If the place has been hit by staff turnover or a lost master, we focus on restoring control without replacing every lock in the building.

For plazas, restaurants, and offices, we also check which doors need to match and which ones should stay separate. That keeps front-of-house, office, storage, and delivery access from getting mixed together. The goal is simple: fewer keys in circulation, tighter control over who opens what, and no unnecessary hardware changes.

Final Checks That Matter

After we rekey, we don't stop at turning pins and handing over keys. We check each cylinder against the right key and the wrong key, because a lock can feel fine and still accept an old key if one stack is off. That matters on commercial doors where staff come and go, records need to stay clean, and one missed lock can leave a back entrance or storage room exposed.

We also test the hardware in place, not just on the bench. A key should turn smoothly with the door open and still work when the door is closed against the strike. If a latch drags, the bolt binds, or a closer is pushing the door out of alignment, we catch it before we leave. Around busy spots like US-40, where doors get used hard and parking lots stay active, that extra check keeps a rekey from becoming a callback later.

When a building uses master keying, we verify the new hierarchy so old masters no longer operate the system and the right personnel still have access where they should. We also confirm every issued key is accounted for and that the lock counts match what was serviced. That last step sounds simple, but it protects you after staff turnover, a lost master, or a handoff between shifts. It gives you a clean starting point, with no guesswork about who can open what.

A standard commercial rekey is the cleanest option when the hardware still works and the concern is who has keys, not what the lock looks like. We change the pins inside the cylinder so old keys stop working and new keys take over. That's the usual move after an employee leaves or a key goes missing, especially when the building has multiple entry points that need the same key.

A master key rekey is different. We keep separate access for individual doors while building one higher-level key for management. That matters in offices and back rooms where not every key should open every lock. If the lock hardware is worn or damaged, rekeying may not solve the problem on its own, and we'll say so. In those cases, the right answer is repair or replacement, not forcing a rekey onto bad parts.

Commercial Lock Rekeying in Glasgow - common questions

How does commercial rekeying work for a shop near People's Plaza?

We open the cylinder, change the internal pins, and cut new keys to match the new setup. The old keys stop working, so you can keep the same lock bodies and still reset access after turnover, a lost key, or a change in who should enter which door.

What should we have ready before you rekey our office or storefront?

Have someone on site who knows which doors should match, which should stay separate, and who needs master access. It also helps if we know whether the locks are all the same brand or if there are mixed locks on the building. Clear access to the affected doors saves confusion.

Can rekeying fix the problem if an old employee still has an office key?

Yes, if the lock is in good shape. Rekeying is the right move when the concern is key control. We reset the cylinder so the old key no longer works. If someone may also have a master key, we need to look at the whole key system, not just one door.

Should we rekey all the doors or just the front entrance after a staff change?

It depends on how the keys are used. If one person had access to only one door, a single rekey may be enough. If the same key opened the front, office, and stock room, it usually makes more sense to rekey the matching locks together so access stays consistent.

What if our building has a mix of old and newer locks that don't all match?

That's common in commercial spaces. We can often rekey the locks that share a keyway, but not every brand or model can be tied into the same system. If some hardware can't be matched cleanly, we'll explain which locks can be grouped and which ones need a different plan.
