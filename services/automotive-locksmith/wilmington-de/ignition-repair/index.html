What Ignition Wear Looks Like

Inside the steering column, the ignition cylinder is the part your key turns. It lines up a series of small pins and springs so the lock can rotate and send power to the electrical switch, letting the vehicle start. When that cylinder is healthy, the key slides in cleanly, turns with steady pressure, and returns the way it should. When it starts to wear, the first signs are usually small: the key feels rough going in, you have to wiggle the wheel or the key to get it to turn, or the cylinder seems to catch in one spot before it releases.

That kind of wear usually comes from years of use, a bent or worn key, dirt inside the cylinder, or damage from forcing the key when the wheel is loaded against the lock. If the key only turns halfway, sticks in the ignition, or needs a little extra force every time, the internal parts may no longer be lining up the way they should. We see this often on older cars and work vehicles around Market Street and over by the Riverfront, where a small starting problem can quickly turn into a no-start situation if the cylinder keeps grinding itself down.

We look at the cylinder, the key, and the surrounding ignition parts to sort out whether the issue is wear inside the lock, a broken component, or a problem that starts elsewhere in the steering column. The goal is to catch it before the key jams or the cylinder stops rotating altogether. If the ignition is still partly working, that's usually the best time to repair it.

Most ignition repair falls into a few real-world patterns. One is a key that goes in but turns rough or hangs in one spot. Another is a cylinder that has worn so much the key will only work after a little wiggling. A third is a break where the key snaps, the cylinder locks up, or the switch won't release the key at all. Each one needs a different fix, even though the driver just feels the same kind of frustration.

The right result is more than getting the car started once. We want the key to insert cleanly, turn with steady pressure, and release the way it should. If the part is too far gone to save, replacing the worn ignition parts is the better call than trying to nurse it along. That's especially true when the problem shows up only after the car's been sitting or after a key has already been cut a few times and worn down.

Ignition Repair in Wilmington - common questions

Why does my ignition turn rough even though my key still works in the door?

That usually means the ignition cylinder is more worn than the door lock, or the key itself has worn down enough to show the problem there first. We check both the key and the cylinder so we can tell whether the issue is in the lock, the key, or both. Forcing it can make the damage worse.

What should I have ready when we call about an ignition problem in Wilmington?

Have the vehicle year, make, and model ready, plus a clear description of what the key is doing. If the key is stuck, broken, or turning partway and stopping, tell us that too. If there's a spare key, that helps us compare the wear and narrow down whether the ignition or the key is the bigger issue.

Can trying to turn a stuck ignition damage my car?

Yes. Forcing a sticky ignition can break the key, bend the cylinder parts, or damage the steering lock and switch. If the key already feels rough, it's better to stop and have it checked. A small mechanical issue can turn into a no-start or a key-removal problem if it gets pushed too far.

Is it better to repair the ignition cylinder or replace it?

It depends on what's worn. If the cylinder is still serviceable, repair can make sense when the problem is limited to worn parts or debris. If the key pattern is badly damaged or the cylinder is binding in multiple spots, replacement is usually the cleaner fix. We look at how the lock behaves before recommending either path.

My key turns but the car still won't start. Is that still an ignition problem?

It can be, but not always. If the key turns normally yet the engine doesn't respond, the issue may be in the ignition switch, the cylinder linkage, or another starting component. We check the whole ignition path so we don't replace the wrong part. A turning key alone doesn't rule anything in or out.
