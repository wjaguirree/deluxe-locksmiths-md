Repair, Rekey, or Replace

When a lock starts sticking, feeling loose, or sitting out of line, we look at what actually failed before we decide anything else. A worn cylinder, a shifted strike, a screw that backed out, or a door that has settled can all make the same lock act up. In a town like Milford, where older homes near Walnut Street sit alongside newer builds and busy household schedules, that kind of wear is common. If the body of the lock is sound, repair is usually the first choice because it keeps the hardware that still has life in it and fixes the part that is causing the trouble.

Rekeying comes into play when the lock works properly but the key control is the issue. That may be after a move, a change in who has access, or when several doors need to match one key. Rekeying does not fix a sticky latch or a misaligned deadbolt, so we only suggest it when the problem is security, not mechanical failure. Replacing the lock makes sense when the parts are worn beyond a reliable repair, the keyway is damaged, or the hardware no longer fits the door the way it should. We see that often on doors that have shifted over time, especially around homes near the Mispillion Riverwalk where weather and settling can both show up at the same time.

Our job is to sort out which path gives you a working lock that stays that way. We check the door, the strike, the latch, and the key action together, then explain whether repair, rekeying, or replacement is the right fix for your situation.

A sticking lock is the most common version of this work. The key may go in, but the cylinder feels rough, the latch drags, or the bolt won't throw cleanly. A loose lock is a different problem. The trim, knob, or deadbolt body may wobble, and that movement can keep the mechanism from engaging fully. A misaligned lock is usually tied to the door itself or the frame, especially after settling or seasonal swelling. In those cases, the repair is about restoring alignment so the lock works without forcing it.

Lock Repair in Milford - common questions

My deadbolt sticks on my Milford front door. Is that usually the lock or the door itself?

It can be either one, and often it's both working against each other. If the door rubs, sags, or shifted with weather, the bolt may be hitting the strike plate instead of sliding in cleanly. If the hardware feels gritty even with the door open, the lock body may need service.

What should I have ready before someone comes out to look at my lock?

Have the door accessible, and be ready to point out exactly what the lock is doing. If you've got the key, an extra copy, or know when the trouble started, that helps. It's also useful to mention any past fixes, like tightening screws or replacing the knob, so the repair starts with good information.

Could a sticky lock on a house near the river get worse if I leave it alone?

Yes. If the issue is alignment, forcing the key or turning the knob hard can wear down the latch, damage the strike, or make the problem harder to correct later. If the door is swelling or settling, the wear can keep building until the lock stops catching reliably.

How do you decide whether to repair the lock or replace it?

If the core problem is a loose mount, a bad strike position, or normal wear, repair is often the better path. Replacement makes more sense when the hardware is broken, badly worn inside, or no longer fits the door well. The decision comes down to what's failing and whether the parts can still do the job properly.

My house is older and the door swells in wet weather. Can the lock still be made to work right?

Usually, yes. Older doors often need a careful adjustment so the lock and strike line up even when the wood moves a little. Sometimes that means tuning the hardware and sometimes it means correcting the door fit. If the door is badly warped, the fix may be limited, but many cases can still be improved.
