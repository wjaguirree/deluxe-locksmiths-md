A lot of people think rekeying means replacing the whole lock, but that's not how it works. If the hardware is sound, we change the pins inside the cylinder so old keys stop working and new ones do. That's often the first move after a move, after a roommate change, or any time you can't be sure who still has a key. For homes in Wilmington, especially the brick rowhouses around Trolley Square, that can keep the original lock in place while giving you a fresh start at the door.

We handle rekeying on site because that's where the work usually makes the most sense. Our job is to match the lock to your current setup, whether it's a standard deadbolt, a knob lock, or an older mortise lock that still has plenty of life left. If a lock is worn out or damaged, we'll tell you that plainly. If it only needs to be reset so the right people can get in, we'll take care of that and leave the hardware working as it should.

Lock rekeying changes which keys work in your existing locks. We keep the hardware in place, rebuild the pinning inside the cylinder, and hand you keys that match the new setup. For a move-in, a lost key, or a rental turnover, that's often the right first step because it gives you control without replacing hardware that still works.

The job goes wrong when someone swaps pins by guesswork, uses the wrong key blanks, or leaves the cylinders rough and sticky after reassembly. That can make a lock bind, wear a key down early, or leave one door behaving differently from the rest. A proper rekey feels ordinary afterward: the old keys stop working, the new ones turn smoothly, and every lock in the set is keyed the way you expected.

In Wilmington, we see this a lot in the brick rowhomes of Trolley Square and Little Italy, where original mortise locks are still in daily use. Those locks need careful disassembly and the right parts put back in the right order. We also see homes where one door was rekeyed before and the others were left behind, which creates confusion for the family. Our goal is to make the system simple again and keep the hardware that already fits the door.

Rekeying Done the Right Way

We see the same shortcuts over and over: people change one front-door cylinder and leave the side door, basement entry, or garage entry on the old key; they pull a set of tumblers and assume the lock is fine; or they swap in a random hardware-store cylinder that never quite matches the door. In a rowhome near Trolley Square or Little Italy, that kind of patchwork leaves a house with more keys in circulation than the owner realizes. One old copy in a drawer, one handed to a contractor, one still fit to the back door, and the whole point of rekeying gets lost.

Another common shortcut is trying to rekey around worn parts instead of fixing what is actually worn. If the plug, springs, or key pins are damaged, the lock may turn today and bind tomorrow. We've also seen people reuse keys that are bent or badly cut, then blame the lock when the problem is the key itself. That leads to sticky operation, broken keys in the cylinder, and a lock that fails at the worst time, usually when someone is headed out toward Market Street or coming home with hands full.

Our approach is to make the existing hardware work cleanly with the right key and to check each door that matters, not just the one that was easy to get to. That matters after a move, after a tenant change, after a lost key, and anytime you want to know exactly who can get in. We keep it practical, match the work to the lock on the door, and leave you with one clear key set instead of a guess.

There are a few common versions of this work. A single-lock rekey is straightforward when one door needs a new key after a move or a key change. A whole-home rekey ties the front, back, and side doors to one key so you're not carrying a ring of mismatched keys. A master-key setup is different again: it lets one key open everything while other keys only open selected doors.

The best choice depends on the locks already on the doors and who needs access. If the house has matching cylinders, we can often align them cleanly. If one lock is older, worn, or not the same type as the others, we may need to talk through what can be matched and what can't. A good result is simple: the right people can get in, the old keys no longer work, and the locks all feel consistent from door to door.

Lock Rekeying in Wilmington - common questions

How does rekeying a house lock work in Wilmington?

We take the lock cylinder apart, change the internal pin pattern, and cut new keys to match that pattern. The lock body stays on the door. That's often the cleanest way to control access after a move, a tenant change, or a lost key, especially when the hardware still fits and works well.

What should I have ready before you rekey my front door?

It helps to know which doors need to match and whether you want one key for the whole house or separate keys for certain doors. If you have any working key now, bring it. If the lock has been hard to turn, mention that so we can check whether rekeying is the right fix or whether the hardware needs attention too.

Can rekeying fix a lock that feels sticky or worn?

Sometimes, but not always. Rekeying changes the key pattern inside the cylinder; it does not repair bent parts, damaged springs, or a door that's out of alignment. If the lock is worn out, we'll say so. A proper rekey should leave the lock turning cleanly, not just differently.

Should I rekey every door or only the one with the lost key?

If one key has gone missing and it opens more than one door, rekeying the full set is usually smarter. If only one door needs attention and the rest are already separate, a single lock may be enough. We can look at how the house is keyed now and help you keep it practical.

Can you rekey older mortise locks in Wilmington rowhomes?

Often, yes. Older mortise locks can usually be serviced if the parts are still sound and the cylinder is the right type. These locks need more care than a basic knob lock, and some badly worn pieces can't be made to behave well. In that case, we'll explain the options before doing any work.
