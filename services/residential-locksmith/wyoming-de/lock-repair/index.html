If the lock is only adjusted in a hurry or forced back into place, the problem tends to come back, and sometimes the hardware gets worn past the point where a simple repair would've been enough.

We work on the lock itself before we talk about replacing it, especially on original mortise hardware and other older residential locks that were built to keep doing their job. Lock repair in Wyoming often comes down to the hardware inside the door, not the knob you see. On older homes here, that can mean a mortise lock set with a case sunk into the edge of the door, a latch bolt, springs, a spindle, and small internal parts that wear, bind, or drift out of line over time. When the door starts sticking, the key turns hard, or the latch won't catch cleanly, the problem is usually one of fit, wear, or alignment rather than a lock that needs to be thrown out.

The job is to find out what's actually failing. A loose faceplate can let the case shift. A worn latch or weak spring can make the handle sag. Old wood doors can move with the seasons and pull the strike out of position. Repair may mean cleaning out hardened debris, tightening the set, rebuilding worn parts, adjusting the strike, or replacing only the damaged internal pieces. If the original hardware is too far gone, a matched replacement can keep the look of the door while restoring solid function.

That matters in Wyoming, where a lot of doors still carry their original hardware and need work that respects the door, not just brute-force replacement. The goal is a lock that closes cleanly, turns smoothly, and holds the door the way it should.

Repair Before Replacement

When a lock starts sticking, feels loose, or won't line up the way it should, it helps to check a few things before we open the door and start work. Tell us whether the trouble is on the deadbolt, knob, or lever, and whether the key turns but won't latch, or the key itself is hard to insert. If you can safely do so, note whether the door swells with weather, drags on the frame, or has sagging hinges. In a lot of older Wyoming homes, that kind of wear shows up first at the door, not the lock, and knowing the difference helps us fix the real cause instead of changing parts that still have life left in them.

Leave the lock itself alone if it's binding. Don't force the key, pry at the cylinder, or add oil, graphite, or spray cleaners unless we've told you to. Those quick fixes can push dirt deeper into the mechanism or hide a worn part that needs to be reset, tightened, or rebuilt. If the problem started after a door was painted, a strike plate shifted, or a new latch was installed, mention that when you call. We often see these issues on houses near DE-10 and around the older streets by the Wyoming Mill Pond, where original hardware and wooden doors are still doing their job. Our goal is to diagnose what's out of alignment, repair what can be repaired, and leave the lock working smoothly without replacing it before we have to.

Lock Repair in Wyoming - common questions

My old front door near the Wyoming Peach Festival grounds sticks when I lock it. Is that the lock or the door?

It can be either one. A sticking door may be caused by a lock case that has shifted, a worn latch, or a strike that no longer lines up. It can also come from the door swelling or dropping a little on its hinges. The repair starts by checking the full setup, not just the knob.

What should I have ready before someone comes out to repair the lock?

Have the key that normally works the lock, and if you've got any old parts from a past repair, keep those nearby. It also helps to know when the problem started and whether the door has been sticking, sagging, or getting harder to turn. That gives a clearer starting point.

If the lock is loose, is it safe to keep using it for now?

Sometimes yes, but a loose lock can get worse fast. The screws may be pulling out, the strike may be shifting, or the latch may stop catching fully. If the door doesn't close securely, it's better to have it looked at before the hardware wears down more or starts jamming.

Should I repair the old mortise lock or replace it with a new one?

If the case is still solid and the important parts can be brought back into shape, repair is often the better path. If the inside is broken, missing pieces, or badly worn, replacement may be the practical choice. On older doors, a matching replacement can preserve the look while restoring proper function.

My back door by DE-10 has original hardware and the key turns, but the latch doesn't always catch. Can that be fixed without changing the whole door?

Usually, yes. That kind of problem often comes from the lock not lining up with the strike, or from a worn latch that no longer throws cleanly. In many cases the door itself can stay as it is. The hardware is checked, adjusted, and repaired where possible before anyone talks about larger changes.
