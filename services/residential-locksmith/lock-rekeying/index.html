Lock Rekeying

Change who can open your doors without replacing the hardware — the first thing to do after a move. Available across Northeast Maryland & Delaware.

Call (443) 545-2775 Request service

We get a lot of calls for rekeying when a house changes hands, especially after work when people finally have a second to think about who still has a key. If you've moved into a place in Cecil County or just taken over a rental, rekeying is usually the first smart step. It keeps the same locks on the doors, but changes which keys will open them, so old copies stop working without replacing all the hardware.

That matters when keys have been passed around to former owners, tenants, contractors, or neighbors. We come to the property, take care of the cylinders on site, and make sure the locks are set up for the people who should actually have access. If the doors are in good shape, rekeying is often the cleanest way to reset the house without changing the look of the lockset. From a townhouse near downtown Elkton to a home out in Harford County or over into Kent County, DE, the goal is the same: keep your doors working and tighten control over who can open them. If you're settling in, or if a key has gone missing and you want a fresh start, we can handle that at the door.

When you actually need this

Rekeying makes sense when the lock itself is still worth keeping, but you need to change who has access. The classic case is right after a move. You may get two keys at closing, but you usually have no clear record of who else still has copies from the seller, a tenant, a dog walker, a cleaner, or a contractor. The same goes for a rental turnover, a breakup, a roommate moving out, or a lost key that could be tied back to your address. If the deadbolt and knob lock are solid, rekeying lets you keep the hardware on the door and reset the keying so the old keys no longer work. It is also a good choice when you want one key to operate several doors, as long as the locks are compatible and in decent condition.

This service is usually the right answer for standard residential hardware that still works normally: single-cylinder deadbolts, keyed entry knobs, keyed levers, and many mailbox or side-door locks. If the lock turns smoothly, the latch lines up, and the key doesn't have to be jiggled or forced, rekeying is often all you need. In older homes around Elkton, it's common to find a front deadbolt keyed one way, a garage entry lever keyed another, and a back door on a third key altogether. Rekeying can bring that under control without changing the look of the house or swapping out trim that already fits the door well.

It is not the best answer when the hardware is worn out or the door has a physical problem. If the key sticks because the cylinder is failing, the latch is dragging, the strike is off, or the bolt doesn't throw cleanly, rekeying won't fix that. A cracked knob, a loose deadbolt, a sagging door, rusted springs, or a cylinder that has been forced are repair or replacement issues. If you have a bargain-grade lock that never worked especially well, replacing it with a better deadbolt is often the smarter move. If you want keypad access, phone control, audit features, or no physical key at all, that points toward smart lock installation instead.

There are also cases where a higher-security upgrade is more important than rekeying alone. If a side door opens to a secluded yard off Pulaski Highway, or if you've had repeated trouble with copied keys changing hands, the better plan may be a high-security cylinder or new deadbolt with restricted key control. Rekeying changes the key pattern, but it does not upgrade weak hardware, improve door alignment, or add features the lock never had. A good locksmith looks at the whole opening, not just the keyway, and tells you whether rekeying solves the actual problem or whether lock repair, replacement, or a new deadbolt will serve you better.

How the work is done

Rekeying starts with identifying exactly what is on the door now, because the right approach depends on the hardware already installed. A basic keyed knob, a deadbolt, a handleset with a separate deadbolt, and a mortise lock all come apart differently and take different keyways and cylinder parts. The first step is to check the brand, keyway, cylinder type, door thickness, handing, and general condition of the lock. If the plug is badly worn, the tailpiece is bent, the latch is dragging, or the deadbolt binds because the door has shifted, that matters before any pinning work begins. A lock can be rekeyed and still feel rough if the problem is really alignment, wear, or a damaged cylinder. On a home near Elkton, it is common to see a mix of old and newer hardware on different doors, so part of the job is deciding which pieces can be keyed together and which cannot.

Once the hardware is evaluated, the cylinder is removed from the lock and taken apart in a controlled way so the keying components can be changed without disturbing springs, clips, cams, or tailpieces that are still serviceable. In most residential pin-tumbler locks, that means repinning the plug to match a new key cut to the correct keyway. The old bottom pins are replaced with a new combination, and the top pins and springs are checked for wear, corrosion, mushrooming, or weak tension. If the lock uses spool or serrated driver pins for added pick resistance, those are noted and kept in the proper chamber positions when appropriate. The goal is not just to make an old key stop working; it is to make the cylinder operate cleanly with proper shear-line alignment and without excess play.

After the cylinder is repinned, the lock is reassembled and tested repeatedly in the hand before it goes back on the door. That test catches common issues such as a key that inserts roughly, a plug that does not return smoothly, or a tailpiece that is not indexed correctly. Then the lock is reinstalled and checked on the actual door with the door open and closed. This matters because plenty of problems only show up under load: a deadbolt that works fine in the air may rub the strike when the door is shut, and a knob latch may stick because the latch face is worn or the edge bore is slightly out of line. If there are multiple exterior doors, rekeying can often be set up so one key works across several locks, provided the cylinders share a compatible keyway and enough pin capacity.

The last part of the work is deciding whether rekeying is the right end point or whether another repair should be done at the same visit. Some locks are good candidates because the hardware is solid and the issue is simply key control after a move, a lost key, or a change in occupancy. Others are not worth repinning because the plug is loose, the bolt throw is inconsistent, the finish has failed and let moisture into the cylinder, or the lock body itself is cracked. On doors in places like Bel Air, a front entry handleset may be rekeyed while a side door deadbolt needs replacement because the cylinder has too much wear to trust. That is the trade-off in practice: rekey the hardware that is still mechanically sound, repair alignment where needed, and replace only the parts that cannot deliver reliable operation.

What it takes to do it properly

A proper rekey starts with knowing exactly what is on the door, not guessing from the key shape. Residential homes around Cecil County, Harford County, and Kent County have a mix of hardware: basic cylindrical knobs, deadbolts, handlesets with separate keyed cylinders, keypad deadbolts, and older mortise locks on some entry doors. Each one takes different parts, different tools, and a different approach at the bench. A locksmith doing the work right carries pin kits matched to the major keyways, replacement top pins and springs, follower tools, plug shims, cap tools, clip removers, gauges, lubricant meant for lock cylinders, and the small parts that keep older hardware from being forced back together. Just as important is having the right blanks and knowing when existing keys are too worn to be trusted as the pattern for the new setup.

The information side matters more than most people realize. Before any pins are changed, the locksmith has to confirm which doors should work on one key, whether garage-entry and side doors should match the front, whether there are patio cylinders or a mailbox lock that the owner wants separated, and whether any existing hardware is worth keeping. They also need to read wear in the key and the cylinder. A lock that has been run for years with a rounded, copied key can have chambers, springs, or the plug itself worn enough that a fresh pinning alone will not give a reliable result. On a Schlage-style cylinder, a Kwikset cylinder, or a small interchangeable core format, the judgement call is different. Rekeying a tired lock to a bad key just bakes the problem in.

What separates careful work from a bodged job is tolerance and testing. Pins have to be set to the correct heights for the chosen key, the plug must turn cleanly at the shear line, and the cylinder has to be reassembled without bent springs, mixed pin sizes, or caps that won't stay put. Then the lock has to be tested in the door, because a cylinder that works in hand can still bind once it is mounted against a misaligned strike or a sagging door. If the deadbolt drags into the frame, people blame the rekey when the real issue is door fit. A proper locksmith checks operation with the door open and closed, watches for key hang-up, and makes sure the thumbturn and latch work as they should.

There is also judgement in deciding when not to rekey. If the hardware is corroded, if a smart lock's keyed backup cylinder is failing, if the tailpiece is damaged, or if a handleset on an older house near US 40 has so much play that the cylinder can't stay stable, replacement may be the better path. The same goes for mismatched brands where making every door take one key would require compromise parts that do not belong in that trim. Good rekeying is not just changing pins. It is matching the new keying plan to the hardware you actually have, correcting small mechanical problems while the lock is apart, and leaving each door with clean operation instead of a key that only works if you jiggle it just right.

Getting it right the first time

Rekeying sounds simple until somebody treats every lock on the door like it's the same cylinder. That's where repeat trips come from. A front entry set might have a deadbolt above a keyed knob, while the side door has a lever, and the back door has an older rim cylinder that takes a different keyway entirely. If the locksmith doesn't identify each cylinder type before the work starts, you can end up with one fresh key that works the deadbolt but not the handle below it, or a storm door cylinder that was missed altogether. Another common problem is assuming worn hardware will behave like new once it's rekeyed. If the plug is scored, the springs are weak, or the tailpiece is bent, changing pins alone won't fix sticky operation. A proper rekey starts with matching the right keyway, checking cylinder condition, and deciding whether the lock is sound enough to reuse.

A lot of trouble shows up after a move, when homeowners want every exterior door put on one key. That can usually be done, but not every brand plays well with every other brand. Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Weiser, and Baldwin all have their own keyways and cylinder formats, and some decorative handlesets hide proprietary parts behind trim. Forcing a "one key for everything" plan onto mixed hardware is a good way to create a second visit. The better approach is to sort the doors into what can be rekeyed together, what needs a cylinder swap, and what should be replaced because the hardware is already failing. Mailbox locks, patio door locks, and garage entry doors also get overlooked. People remember the front door and forget the service door until they're standing outside with the wrong key.

The small details matter too. Handing over keys that were cut from a worn copy instead of the original code can leave a fresh rekey feeling rough from day one. So can skipping door alignment checks. A deadbolt that drags because the door has settled is still going to drag after rekeying, and that wear gets blamed on the key. On older homes near Main Street, it's common to see a lock that technically works but needs the strike adjusted, the latch centered, or the cylinder tightened so the key turns cleanly. Good service means testing each lock with the door open and closed, checking that the latch retracts fully, and making sure the thumbturn and outside key agree with each other.

Avoiding a callback comes down to doing the whole job, not just the pinning. That means confirming every door the customer wants covered, matching keys only where the hardware allows it, replacing problem cylinders when rekeying won't solve the underlying wear, and cutting clean working keys from the right source. It also means checking related hardware like deadbolts, latch mechanisms, and strike plates so the lock isn't being asked to overcome a door problem. When that inspection is done up front, the rekey holds up better and the customer doesn't find out later that one side entrance by Route 40 was never really brought into the plan.

What to have ready

Before you call about rekeying, it helps to walk the property once and note every door you actually use, not just the front entry. Include the garage service door, side door, basement door, storm door if it has a keyed cylinder, and any door from the garage into the house. If you have a mailbox with a key, a shed, or a detached workshop, mention those too if you want them handled at the same visit. Check whether the locks are deadbolts, keyed knobs, lever locks, mortise locks, or a handleset with a separate deadbolt above. Many homes have mixed brands and mixed keyways, and that matters because some locks can be rekeyed to match while others cannot. If you know brand names like Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Baldwin, Defiant, or Emtek, have that ready, but don't worry if you don't. A few clear phone photos of the inside and outside trim can answer a lot before anyone comes out.

It also helps to know what problem you're trying to solve. After a move, the usual goal is simple: old keys should stop working, and the household should be reduced to as few keys as practical. Say whether you want every exterior door on one key or whether you prefer separate keys for certain doors. There are trade-offs. One key for everything is convenient, but some people want a back door or rental space keyed differently. Mention if any lock is already acting up. Common issues are a key that has to be jiggled, a cylinder that turns roughly, a thumbturn that binds, loose trim, a latch that sticks because the door is sagging, or a deadbolt that only throws cleanly when the door is pushed or lifted. Rekeying changes the internal keying; it does not fix worn hardware, a cracked tailpiece, a misaligned strike, or a latch that is failing.

If you still have any working keys, gather them in one place before the appointment, including copies that previous owners, contractors, neighbors, or family members may have had. If there's a key that works one lock but not another, keep that separate and label it. If a lock has no working key, say so ahead of time. Also note any smart lock on the door. Some smart locks still use a standard key cylinder that can be rekeyed, while others may call for a different approach depending on the model. If there is a double-cylinder deadbolt that needs a key on both sides, mention that as well. For homes near Main Street with older doors, it is not unusual to find older mortise hardware or cylinders that have been replaced piecemeal over the years.

On the day itself, make sure there is clear access to each door and that pets are secured away from the work area. Someone with authority to approve the work should be present, because questions often come up once the cylinders are out and the hardware can be inspected closely. The locksmith will normally verify which locks are being rekeyed, check cylinder condition, confirm what can be matched onto the same key, and point out any hardware that is too worn, damaged, or incompatible to give reliable results. In places such as Bel Air, newer subdivisions often have builder-grade locks that rekey easily, while older homes may have a mix of replacement parts from different eras. Once the work is done, test every new key in every lock before the visit ends, including from both sides where applicable, so you know the turn is smooth and the latch and deadbolt are operating the way they should.

Frequently asked questions

Can you rekey every lock in the house to work with one key, or are there limits?

Often, yes, but it depends on the lock brand, keyway, and condition of the cylinders already installed. Two locks that look similar on the door may be built differently inside and may not accept the same key blank. If a lock is badly worn or a part is missing, rekeying it may not give reliable results. In those cases, keeping a small second key or replacing one cylinder can make more sense.

If I moved in and the locks work fine, is rekeying still worth doing instead of waiting until something goes wrong?

Yes. A working lock only tells you the hardware still turns; it does not tell you how many old keys are still out there. Previous owners, tenants, contractors, dog walkers, house cleaners, or neighbors may still have copies. Rekeying resets control of the home without changing every knob or deadbolt. It also gives you a good time to check which doors should share a key and which ones should stay separate.

Do smart locks and keypad deadbolts still need to be rekeyed, or does changing the code handle that?

Changing the code only affects keypad access. If the lock also has a physical keyway, any old metal key that matches that cylinder can still open it until the cylinder is rekeyed. That matters after a move, a breakup, a lost key, or a contractor handoff. Some electronic locks use standard cylinders and rekey normally. Others use brand-specific parts, so the right approach depends on the exact model on your door.

Should I rekey side doors, the garage entry, and older exterior locks too, or just the main front door?

Rekeying only the front door leaves other entry points unchanged, so most homeowners choose to include every exterior door that can be opened with a key. That usually means the back door, side door, garage service door, and sometimes a storm door or shed if security matters there. Older locks should be checked before rekeying. If a cylinder sticks, binds, or has heavy wear, rekeying may not be the best long-term fix.
