Door Lock Repair

Door hardware realigned and repaired so the latch catches smoothly again instead of being shouldered shut. Available across Northeast Maryland & Delaware.

Will the lock actually line up with the door, or are we just forcing the latch until it catches?

When a door starts sticking, rubbing, or needing a hard shoulder to shut, the lock often isn't the real problem by itself. The strike plate may have shifted, the latch may be worn, or the door and frame may no longer be meeting the way they should. We repair and realign residential door hardware so the latch catches cleanly again and the door closes the way it's supposed to. That can mean adjusting the strike, fixing worn parts, tightening loose hardware, or sorting out a door that has settled over time. We do this work on site for homes across Cecil County, Harford County, and Kent County, because most of the problem starts at the door, not at a shop counter.

If the lock is binding, the key turns but nothing feels right, or you're having to slam the door just to get it shut, we can take a look and repair what's actually causing the trouble. We work on entry doors, side doors, and other common residential hardware, and we aim to leave you with a lock that lines up and operates smoothly again. From homes near Elkton to places farther out in the region, we handle these repairs where you are, with the goal of making the door close normally and stay that way.

When you actually need this

Door lock repair is the right call when the lock still has a useful life in it, but the door and hardware have drifted out of alignment or parts inside the lock are no longer moving cleanly. A common example is a latch that hits the strike plate instead of entering it, so the door has to be pushed, lifted, or shouldered before it will stay shut. Another is a deadbolt that only turns when the door is held in a certain position. On older wood doors, seasonal swelling, hinge sag, and loose screws can move the door just enough to make a good lock feel bad. On newer doors, the issue may be a mislocated strike, a warped slab, or a cylindrical lockset that was installed slightly off. In those cases, repairing and realigning the hardware is usually more sensible than swapping out every part on the door.

You also need lock repair when the key works, but the lock feels rough, sticky, or inconsistent. That can point to worn pins, a tired spring, a tailpiece that is slipping, a latch assembly that is binding, or a thumbturn that no longer retracts the bolt smoothly. We see this with keyed knobs, lever locksets, single-cylinder deadbolts, mortise locks, and some electronic deadbolts where the mechanical side is fine one day and unreliable the next. If the door closes but bounces back open, the latch may not be projecting fully. If the deadbolt scrapes or leaves bright wear marks on the strike, the bolt may be entering at the wrong height. On a house near Route 40, that can look like a lock problem to the homeowner when the root cause is really the door frame settling.

But repair is not always the best answer. If the lock has been forced, the bolt is bent, the faceplate is cracked, the cylinder housing is damaged, or the screw holes are stripped beyond a solid reset, replacement is usually the better path. The same goes for very low-grade hardware that has reached the end of its service life, or mismatched parts that were pieced together over time. If your concern is key control after a move, a breakup, a contractor change, or a lost key, rekeying is often the right service instead of repair. If you want stronger throw, better resistance to wear, or a different function at the door, replacing the lock or installing a proper deadbolt may solve more than another adjustment would.

Sometimes the symptom points to a different service entirely. A lock that works mechanically but no longer matches the rest of the house may call for lock replacement. A smart lock that has power and setup issues, but a sound door and frame, may need smart lock installation or correction rather than basic repair. A front door in downtown Elkton that never latches unless it is pulled hard may need hinge correction, strike relocation, latch replacement, or a combination of small repairs rather than a new cylinder. The point of this service is to get the existing door and lock working together again: smooth latch engagement, full bolt throw, clean key operation, and a door that closes and stays secure without force.

How the work is done

Most door lock repair starts with figuring out whether the problem is in the lock itself, the door, or the frame. A front door that has to be pushed, lifted, or shouldered shut usually has more than one issue going on at once. The first step is checking door swing, hinge wear, latch alignment, and the gap around the slab. Seasonal movement, loose hinge screws, a sagging door, or a strike plate that drifted out of line can all make a good lock feel like a bad one. The lock is then tested with the door open and closed so it's clear whether the sticking is inside the cylinder, in the latch or deadbolt, or where the bolt meets the strike. On homes around Elkton, that difference matters, because replacing parts that aren't causing the bind won't fix a door that keeps fighting the frame.

Once the source is identified, the hardware comes apart far enough to inspect the working parts without turning the job into a full replacement unless that's actually needed. On a cylindrical knob or lever set, that may mean removing trim, through-bolts, chassis, latch, and strike so worn springs, a loose retractor, or a bent latch tongue can be checked. On a single-cylinder deadbolt, the thumbturn side, keyed cylinder, tailpiece, and bolt housing are inspected for play, drag, and mounting stress. Screws that are over-tightened can pinch the chassis and make the key hard to turn; screws that are loose can let the lock shift every time the door closes. If the cylinder is rough, it may be serviced or rekeyed depending on wear and the homeowner's goals. If the latch faceplate is proud of the door edge, or the cross bore has wallowed out, the repair has to address that fit, not just the visible trim.

The door and frame are then corrected so the latch catches smoothly again. That can involve resetting hinges, replacing short hinge screws with proper anchors into solid framing, adjusting the strike position, or dressing a misfit mortise so the latch and deadbolt sit square. A dead latch, the small auxiliary plunger beside the spring latch on many entry sets, has to contact the strike properly or the lock won't behave as designed. Strike openings are checked for depth and width so the latch isn't rubbing wood, metal, or a warped weatherstrip. If the problem is a split jamb, crushed screw holes, or a strike that's been moved too many times, the repair may include reinforcing the area before reinstalling hardware. On doors near Route 40 that see a lot of daily use, this frame work is often the difference between a repair that lasts and one that slips back out of alignment.

Before the job is wrapped up, everything is reassembled, tightened to the right tension, lubricated where appropriate, and tested repeatedly from both sides. The key should turn without binding, the thumbturn should throw the bolt cleanly, and the latch should engage the strike without needing force on the door. If the existing hardware is too worn, the wrong backset, or simply not suited to the door, that's when replacement options make sense, whether that means a new deadbolt, a better grade knob or lever, or moving to a smart lock or high-security cylinder. The goal of the repair is straightforward: a door that closes normally, lines up with the frame, and locks the way it should without guesswork or extra pressure.

What it takes to do it properly

A good door lock repair starts before any screws come out. The job needs the right hand tools, but it also needs the right measuring and diagnostic gear: a latch gauge, calipers, hinge shims, longer strike screws, wood repair material for wallowed-out screw holes, a selection of strikes and faceplates, and enough common residential parts to match what is already on the door. On a service call, the first question is not "which lock is bad," but "what changed." A deadbolt that used to turn cleanly and now binds may be fine internally, while the door has dropped, the weatherstrip has swollen, the hinges have loosened, or the strike has shifted just enough that the bolt is scraping metal on every throw. A knob or lever that feels sticky can be suffering from worn return springs, a bent spindle, a mis-set backset, paint packed into the latch edge, or simple misalignment between latch and strike.

Doing it properly means reading the whole door as a system. You check reveal lines, hinge condition, latch position, strike depth, and whether the door is rubbing at the head or jamb. You identify the hardware type because each one has its own failure pattern: tubular latches wear differently than mortise locks; cylindrical deadbolts react differently to frame movement than interconnected locksets; smart locks add battery, calibration, and handing issues that a standard keyed entry set does not. You also need the basic information that homeowners often have on hand but do not realize matters: whether the problem started after new flooring, new weatherstripping, repainting, a storm, or a forced push against a stuck door. On older homes around Elkton, settled frames and repaired jambs can hide the real cause unless someone checks beyond the trim plate.

Judgement is what separates a proper repair from a bodged one. Anyone can file a strike opening bigger or crank down random screws until the latch sort of catches. That may quiet the symptom for a while, but it often leaves a door that has to be shouldered shut, a deadbolt that only works with pressure on the knob, or a latch that barely holds because the bevel and strike lip are fighting each other. A sound repair keeps security, fire separation, and daily usability in balance. That can mean resetting the strike instead of over-cutting it, rebuilding stripped wood so screws bite again, shimming a hinge to correct the swing, or replacing only the worn internal part rather than the whole trim set. It can also mean recognizing when a decorative handle is not the locking component at all, and the real problem is in the separate deadbolt above it.

The finished result should feel ordinary in the best way. The door closes without a slam, the latch catches smoothly, the key turns without forcing, and the deadbolt throws cleanly with the door in its natural position. There should not be ragged metal, oversized holes, chewed screw heads, or mismatched hardware used as a shortcut. In places that see damp air off the Susquehanna, corrosion and swelling can return if the repair ignores material condition, so part of doing it right is choosing parts and adjustments that will hold up rather than just getting the door to behave for the moment. That is the difference between a repair that restores the lock and door to proper working order and one that merely hides the problem until the next hard close.

Getting it right the first time

A door lock repair only holds up if the whole opening is checked, not just the cylinder or the latch that seems to be acting up. A lot of repeat problems start when someone replaces one part and assumes the rest is fine. The key may turn, but the latch is still dragging on the strike, the deadbolt is entering the pocket at an angle, or the hinges have let the slab sag just enough to throw everything out of line. On wood doors, seasonal movement can tighten the margin at the top corner and shift the latch path. On steel or fiberglass doors, loose mounting screws, compressed weatherstrip, or a twisted strike can create the same symptom. If you only tighten a faceplate and leave the door rubbing the frame, the customer ends up shouldering it shut again a short time later.

Another common mistake is treating every sticky lock like a lubrication issue. Dry graphite, PTFE, and light lock lubricant all have their place, but they do not fix worn pins, a tired tailpiece, a bent latchbolt, or a mis-sized replacement part. On tubular knob and lever sets, the spring latch can lose tension or bind because the chassis is failing. On a deadbolt, the bolt may retract fine with the door open but bind under pressure once the door is closed, which points to alignment rather than the plug itself. Mortise hardware brings its own problems: hub wear, loose trim, and internal case damage can mimic a bad cylinder. Getting it right means checking operation with the door open and closed, reading witness marks on the strike and edge, and verifying that the hardware matches the door prep instead of forcing a near-fit.

Second visits also happen when the repair ignores the fasteners and the door structure. Short screws in a strike plate, stripped wood at the jamb, and ovaled-out holes behind a deadbolt all let the hardware shift after the job is done. A proper repair may involve re-securing the strike with the correct screws, stabilizing the mounting points, shimming hinges where needed, and making sure the latch enters cleanly without riding the lip of the strike. On some doors, especially older ones near Elkton, the problem is cumulative: a little hinge wear, a little frame movement, and a replacement knobset that was never adjusted quite right. If those small faults are left in place, the lock gets blamed again.

The trade-off is simple: a quick part swap can restore basic function, but a durable repair usually means taking time to inspect the latch, deadbolt, strike, hinges, backset, door bevel, and handing as one system. That is how you avoid the cycle where a lock works for a short stretch and then starts sticking, scraping, or refusing to catch. Sometimes the right answer is repair and realignment. Sometimes worn hardware should be replaced, then keyed to match the house through rekeying so the customer is not juggling extra keys. The point is to solve the reason the lock failed, not just the symptom that showed up that day.

What to have ready

Before you call about a door lock that isn't catching right, it helps to know exactly which door is giving you trouble and what the lock is doing. Front entry, side door from the garage, storm door, basement walkout, and a door between the house and an attached garage can all fail in different ways. If you can, look at the hardware and note what type you have: a standard keyed knob, keyed lever, single-cylinder deadbolt, double-cylinder deadbolt, mortise lock, handleset, or electronic keypad lock. Check whether the key turns normally, whether the thumbturn binds, whether the latch sticks partway out, and whether the deadbolt scrapes or stops before it reaches full throw. It also helps to say whether the problem started suddenly after a hard slam, showed up during humid weather, or has been getting worse over time. Those details point to different causes, from loose hinges and a sagging door to a worn latch, misaligned strike plate, swollen wood, or a cylinder that's starting to fail.

If you're able to do a quick visual check, gather the basics without taking anything apart. Look for missing screws on the hinges or strike plate, a faceplate that has shifted, or rub marks on the edge of the door and frame. If the door only closes when you lift it, shoulder it, or pull it tight, say that. If it latches when open but not when shut, that matters too. Have the brand name ready if you can see it on the inside trim or faceplate, especially with Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Baldwin, Defiant, or an older mortise setup where parts and adjustment options vary. If the lock has been rekeyed before, if the door was recently painted, or if another contractor replaced weatherstripping, mention that as well. Those changes can affect clearances more than people expect.

On the day of service, make sure the locksmith can reach the problem door and work both sides of it. Move shoes, packages, mats, pet gates, or furniture away from the swing path. If there's a storm door, security door, or a closer attached, point that out at the start because it changes how the door can be tested and adjusted. Keep any keys that still work nearby, along with any key that sticks or only works from one side. If more than one household member has noticed different symptoms, it helps to say so. One person may notice the key dragging, while another notices the latch bouncing off the strike unless the door is pushed.

The visit usually starts with checking the door, frame, hinges, latch, strike, and bolt alignment together rather than focusing on the key alone. The locksmith will open and close the door repeatedly, test the lock under normal pressure, and look for where the hardware is out of line or worn. In many cases the repair is a matter of realigning hardware, correcting the strike position, tightening or replacing fasteners, adjusting a latch, or replacing a damaged lock body or cylinder when wear is too far along. If the lock is repairable but the key operation is rough because of age or internal wear, you may also be given the choice between repairing what's there, rekeying related locks to keep one working key, or replacing the hardware with a better-matched deadbolt or handleset. The goal is a door that closes and latches smoothly without forcing it shut.

Frequently asked questions

How can I tell whether the problem is the lock itself or the door being out of alignment?

A bad lock and a misaligned door can feel similar, but the clues are a little different. If the key turns stiffly even with the door open, the cylinder or internal parts may be worn. If the key works better when you pull, lift, or push the door, the latch and strike are likely out of line. You may also see scrape marks on the strike plate or a latch that barely catches unless the door is forced shut.

Can a lock be repaired, or does it usually make more sense to replace it?

It depends on what failed. A lot of issues come from loose hardware, a sagging door, a strike plate set wrong, or parts inside the lock that can be cleaned, adjusted, or replaced. Repair is not always the right move, though. If the lock body is cracked, the cylinder is badly worn, or the hardware has been forced, replacement is often the better long-term fix. On older decorative sets, keeping the existing trim may still be possible.

Will fixing the lock also stop the door from sticking or needing to be shoved closed?

Often, yes, but only if the real cause is alignment. When a door has to be shouldered shut, the latch may be hitting the strike plate wrong, the hinges may be loose, or the door may have shifted with wear or seasonal movement. A proper repair can include tightening hardware, adjusting the strike, and correcting how the latch meets the frame. If the slab or frame is warped, though, lock repair alone may not solve everything.

Is it a bad idea to keep using a lock that works only if I jiggle the key or lift the handle?

Yes. That kind of problem usually gets worse, not better. Repeatedly forcing the key, twisting the knob hard, or lifting the handle to make the latch catch can wear the cylinder, damage the latch, or loosen the mounting screws in the door. It can also leave you locked out or unable to secure the house. Until it is repaired, use a different entrance if you have one and avoid forcing the hardware.
