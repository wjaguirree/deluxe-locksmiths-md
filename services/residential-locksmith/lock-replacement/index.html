Lock Replacement

Worn, outdated or compromised locks swapped for solid modern hardware. Available across Northeast Maryland & Delaware.

Call (443) 545-2775 Request service

A lot of people think a sticky key means the lock just needs to be worked back and forth, or that the whole door has to be replaced. Usually it's the hardware that's worn out, out of date, or no longer holding up the way it should. When a lock starts binding, spinning, or feeling loose, replacing it can be the cleaner fix. We replace damaged or outdated residential locks with solid modern hardware that fits the door and gives the home a more dependable layer of security.

We handle that work where you are, whether the problem is at a house in Elkton or somewhere else in Cecil County, Harford County, New Castle County, or Kent County. Our goal is straightforward: remove the lock that isn't doing its job and install one that does. If a door has been forced, a key has gone missing, or you just don't trust the old hardware anymore, we can help you get it sorted. We work on the spot, keep the process practical, and make sure the new lock is ready to use before we leave.

When you actually need this

Lock replacement makes sense when the hardware itself is the problem, not just the key. A deadbolt that binds, a knob lock with a loose cylinder, a tailpiece that slips, or a latch that no longer retracts cleanly are signs that wear has moved past a simple adjustment. You also replace locks when the existing hardware is outdated for the door and the risk involved. A basic keyed knob on an exterior door is not the same thing as a solid single-cylinder deadbolt with a proper strike and longer screws into the framing. If the lock was forced, if the cylinder was damaged by an attempted break-in, or if the keyway is worn enough that keys have to be jiggled to work, swapping the whole unit is often the cleaner answer.

A change in control is another big reason. If you moved into a house and don't know who still has copies, replacing the locks gives you a fresh starting point, especially if you also want different hardware than what's there now. The same applies after a lost key, a tenant turnover, a breakup, or a house key that was handed out too widely over the years. In some cases, rekeying is enough, and that's usually the better option if the lock body is solid, the brand is decent, and you want to keep the existing finish and style. Rekeying changes which key operates the lock without replacing the exterior trim, but it only makes sense when the lock you already have is worth keeping.

There are also cases where another service is the better fit. If the lock works but the door has dropped, rubs the frame, or needs to be pushed or pulled to latch, that points to alignment and door condition, not necessarily bad hardware. A door lock repair may solve a sticky latch, weak return spring, or a strike issue without changing every piece. If you want stronger security rather than just a fresh key, deadbolt installation or high-security lock installation may be the right move instead of replacing a like-for-like knob set. If you're adding keypad access for a side entry near Route 40, that falls under smart lock installation more than ordinary replacement.

Mailbox and interior locks are another place where the right call depends on the failure. A mailbox cam lock with a bent pawl usually gets replaced because the parts are small and not built for major repair. A bedroom or bathroom privacy lock that has a cracked chassis is often better replaced than nursed along. On the other hand, if your front door has good hardware from Schlage or Kwikset and the issue is a worn key or poor alignment, a targeted repair or rekey can be the smarter choice. The goal is not to replace parts for the sake of it. It's to match the fix to the actual condition of the lock, the door, and how much control and security you need at the property.

How the work is done

Most lock replacement jobs start with the door and frame, not the new hardware. The first step is figuring out what failed and what the opening will actually support. A worn key cylinder is one problem, but a loose bore hole, a split jamb, a sagging door, or a deadbolt that has been dragging for years can make a new lock perform badly if those issues are ignored. On a house in Elkton, that often means checking whether the latch is striking cleanly, whether the backset is 2-3/8 inch or 2-3/4 inch, and whether the existing prep fits a standard tubular latch or an older mortise pattern. Entry doors, side doors, storm-and-wood door combinations, garage entry doors, and mailbox locks all have different hardware limits, so the replacement is chosen to match the opening instead of forcing a one-size-fits-all setup.

Once the old hardware is removed, the condition of the cross bore, edge bore, latch pocket, and strike area matters. If screw holes are wallowed out, they may need to be stabilized so the new lock body sits tight and the screws hold under daily use. If the old deadbolt used a short throw or a light-duty chassis, it may be replaced with a Grade 2 or Grade 1 style deadbolt depending on the door and the customer's priorities. Some homes are better served by a standard keyed knob paired with a separate deadbolt. Others do better with a lever set for easier grip, especially on a rear entry or interior garage door. If the customer wants one key to operate several doors, cylinders can be matched to the same keyway when the hardware line allows it. If key control matters more, a restricted or high-security cylinder may make sense, with the trade-off that future copies and replacement parts are more specialized.

Installation is more than swapping trim. The latch or deadbolt has to seat flush in the edge of the door, the tailpiece has to be oriented correctly, and the inside and outside assemblies have to clamp evenly without binding the cylinder. Strike placement is critical. A lock that feels stiff is often fighting door alignment, not suffering from a bad key. The strike may need to be shifted, deepened, or reinforced with longer screws into solid framing so the bolt enters fully and the door closes without pressure. On older homes near Bel Air, it is common to see paint buildup, seasonal swelling, or slight frame movement that has to be corrected during the replacement so the new hardware does not inherit the same problems.

After the hardware is set, the lock is tested through a full cycle with the door open and closed. That includes checking key operation from both sides where applicable, verifying that the deadbolt throw is full, making sure the latch retracts cleanly, and confirming that the thumbturn or interior lever is not rubbing the trim. Smart lock replacements add another layer: the door must align well enough for the motor to throw the bolt without strain, the interior escutcheon has to clear the door and frame, and handing must be set correctly so the software and hardware agree. The last part of the job is practical cleanup and handoff: making sure the screws are secure, the finish is seated neatly against the door, the customer has the right keys, and the new lock is doing what it is supposed to do every time the door is used.

What it takes to do it properly

A proper lock replacement starts before any screws come out. The door has to be identified correctly: wood, steel, fiberglass, storm-door setup, older mortise prep, standard cylindrical bore, or a narrow stile that limits hardware choices. The existing backset, cross bore, edge bore, latch faceplate, strike size, and door thickness all matter. So does handing, especially on interconnected locks and some electronic trim. Good work means showing up with more than one finish, latch length, strike pattern, and cylinder format, because homes around Elkton and the surrounding counties are a mix of newer prehung doors, older solid wood doors, and patched-up entries that don't match catalog assumptions.

The hardware itself is only part of the job. The person replacing it needs to read what failed and why. A lock that feels rough may have worn pins and a tired tailpiece, but it can also be a sagging slab pulling the latch against the strike, a split jamb, loose hinge screws, or a deadbolt thrown into a hole that was drilled off center years ago. Swapping in new hardware without correcting that bind is how a fresh lock starts acting old right away. Sometimes the right answer is a grade better deadbolt, a longer throw latch, a reinforced strike, or a cylinder keyed to match other doors. Other times it's knowing that a smart lock is a bad fit for a warped door that already needs pressure to latch.

What separates a proper result from a bodged one is alignment and prep. The latch should enter cleanly without scraping. The deadbolt should project fully and retract without forcing the thumbturn. Screws need to be the right length and driven into solid material, not stripped holes. Faceplates should sit flush instead of rocking on paint ridges or oversized mortises. On replacement work, older holes often need careful correction so the new trim covers properly and the spindle, tailpiece, and latch all meet on center. If the strike is left too low, the bolt drags. If the bore is chewed up and not stabilized, the lock loosens over time. If the wrong cylinder length is used on a thick decorative door, the keyway sits awkwardly and the trim never tightens as it should.

Judgment is the part people don't see. There's a trade-off between keeping a familiar key system, upgrading to higher security hardware, matching existing finishes, and choosing something that will hold up to daily use at a front entry near busy roads like US 40. A good replacement should suit the door, the frame, and the way the house is used. It should lock smoothly, seat firmly, and make sense for the people carrying the keys. That's the difference between simply changing parts and leaving behind a lock that actually works the way it should.

Getting it right the first time

A lock replacement goes wrong when someone treats the cylinder as the whole job and ignores the door around it. The new hardware may be a good brand and still work poorly if the backset is wrong, the latch face doesn't match the existing mortise, or the bore hole is worn out from years of use. On older wood doors, the cross bore can be slightly oversized or out of round, which lets a knobset or deadbolt shift under load. That movement turns into sticking, loose trim, screws that back out, and a latch that drags instead of entering the strike cleanly. The right first visit starts with measuring the door thickness, checking the bore spacing, confirming the hand of the door when it matters, and looking at how the door actually closes in the frame before any replacement hardware goes on.

A lot of second visits happen because the lock was chosen without matching it to the real use of the opening. A front entry that sees daily traffic may need a proper deadbolt with a reinforced strike, not just a light-duty keyed knob. A side door exposed to weather may be better served by hardware with stronger corrosion resistance and a finish that holds up, rather than whatever is on the truck shelf. If the existing setup is a handleset, the through-bolt spacing has to line up or the installer ends up covering old holes with trim that never sits right. If the customer wants smart lock features, the condition of the door matters as much as the electronics. Many battery-powered smart locks are less tolerant of a door that binds, a sagging slab, or a latch that needs force to retract. In those cases, the professional fix is often to correct alignment first so the lock isn't being asked to overcome a mechanical problem every single day.

Strike alignment is where good lock replacement work really shows. A deadbolt should throw fully into a properly positioned strike, not scrape the lip, hit wood, or depend on the door being pulled hard to one side. Misalignment causes hard key turns, premature wear on the tailpiece, and complaints that the lock is "bad" when the real issue is the frame. On a steel door, the problem may be shallow anchoring or a warped weatherstrip pressing the slab out of position. On a wood jamb, the screw holes may be stripped or the jamb itself may be split behind the strike. Replacing the visible hardware without addressing those conditions is how you end up back at the same house. Around Elkton, that's especially common on homes where seasonal movement changes the gap at the head and latch side.

Getting it right the first time also means deciding what should be replaced and what should be rekeyed so the whole house works together. If one deadbolt is compromised but the other openings are sound, it may make sense to install new hardware at the problem door and key the rest to match. If the existing latchsets are worn, have sloppy plug rotation, or use lightweight components that have already started to fail, piecemeal work can leave the homeowner with a mixed system that never feels consistent. A careful replacement visit checks keyway compatibility, verifies that interior thumbturns clear storm-door hardware, and makes sure the new trim sits flat on the door skin instead of rocking on old paint ridges or scarred wood. Small details like that are what prevent callbacks after a lock change on a house near US-40 or anywhere else in the service area.

What to have ready

Before you call, it helps to know exactly which door or doors need new hardware and what is going wrong. A front entry deadbolt that sticks is a different job from a patio handle set with a worn latch, and both are different from an old keyed knob on a side door that no longer turns cleanly. If a key was lost, a tenant moved out, or the lock has signs of tampering, say that up front so the replacement is planned around security rather than just wear. If you can, count how many keyed cylinders are involved and note whether you want one key to work every exterior door or separate keys for separate doors. Also check whether the door is wood, steel, or fiberglass, and whether it closes squarely into the frame. A good lock won't perform right on a sagging or misaligned door.

A few photos are useful if you have them. Try to get the inside trim, the outside trim, the edge of the door where the latch sits, and the strike area on the frame. That helps identify whether you have a standard bored lock, a single-cylinder deadbolt, a mortise setup, a handleset, or something less common. If you know a brand name such as Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Baldwin, or Emtek, mention it, but don't worry if you don't. It also helps to know whether the door has a standard backset and bore or whether an existing hole pattern may limit replacement options. If you're interested in a smart lock, be ready to say whether you want a keypad only or app control, and whether there is a storm door, thick trim, or a narrow stile that might affect fit.

Have proof that you live at or manage the property, especially if the locks are being replaced after a move-out, break-in concern, or change in occupancy. For a house, that may be a photo ID with the address, closing paperwork, or a lease. For a rental or managed property, it helps to have the owner or property manager's contact information ready so there's no confusion about authorization. If there are gate codes, pets in the home, alarm contacts on the door, or a door that swells in damp weather, say so. On older homes, mention any loose trim, oversized holes, split jamb wood, or doors that rub near the strike. Those details matter because sometimes the lock is not the only failed part.

On the day of service, the work usually starts with a close look at the door, frame, latch, strike, and existing prep before any new hardware is installed. If the current lock is simply worn out, replacement may be straightforward. If the bolt has been dragging, the strike may need adjustment, the latch pocket may need cleanup, or the door edge may need a better-fitting faceplate so the new hardware isn't fighting the opening every time you use it. You can expect a discussion of practical options: standard pin-tumbler hardware, a stronger deadbolt, a keyed entry lever instead of a knob, or a smart lock where it makes sense. Once the right hardware is chosen, the old parts are removed, the new lock is fitted and aligned, keys are checked, and the door is tested for smooth operation from both sides. Before the job wraps up, you'll be shown how the new lock works, what key goes where if multiple cylinders were changed, and whether any follow-up repair to the door or frame would help the new hardware last.

Frequently asked questions

If I replace one exterior lock, should I replace the rest so everything matches the same key?

Usually, yes. If your front door, back door, and side entry all take different keys, daily use gets annoying and it also makes key control harder. In many homes, new hardware can be set up to work on one key as long as the lock types are compatible. If they are not, it may make more sense to replace a group of locks together instead of mixing old worn parts with new ones.

Can my existing deadbolt be reused if the door is sagging or the latch doesn't line up well?

Sometimes the lock is not the main problem. If the door is rubbing, the strike is off, or the frame has shifted, reusing the deadbolt may keep the same binding and uneven wear. A replacement works best when the door closes squarely and the latch enters the strike cleanly. If alignment is poor, the door or strike position may need to be corrected along with the lock so the new hardware does its job.

What should I do if I want better security but don't want smart locks or app-based hardware?

That's a common request. You can stay with fully mechanical hardware and still improve security. A solid single-cylinder deadbolt, a reinforced strike area, longer mounting screws where appropriate, and fresh hardware that fits the door correctly can be a real upgrade over worn builder-grade locks. For many homeowners, reliability matters more than phone control. A good replacement can keep things simple, durable, and easier to maintain over time.

Is lock replacement the right move after a break-in attempt, or is rekeying enough?

It depends on what the lock went through. If the key was lost but the hardware is still in good shape, rekeying may solve the problem. If the cylinder was forced, the thumbturn feels loose, the bolt drags, or the face of the lock shows pry or drill damage, replacement is usually the better choice. Damage around the strike or door edge should also be checked, because a new lock cannot fix a weakened door by itself.
