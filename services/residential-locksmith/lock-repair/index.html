Lock Repair

Sticking, loose or misaligned locks diagnosed and fixed instead of needlessly replaced. Available across Northeast Maryland & Delaware.

Call (443) 545-2775 Request service

A lock that sticks, wobbles, or won't line up right can turn an ordinary door into a daily problem. You shouldn't have to force the key, shoulder the latch, or keep jiggling the handle just to get in. When a repair is done wrong, the issue usually comes right back, or the door starts wearing harder against the frame and the hardware gets worse. We fix the lock as a working part of the door, not just the part you can see, so the repair holds up to regular use.

Most of this work is done at the home, which matters when the door, the strike, or the hardware needs to be checked together. In places like Cecil County and across Kent County, we see locks that are loose from use, out of line after a door shift, or damaged from weather and everyday wear. If the body of the lock can still be saved, we repair it instead of pushing a replacement that doesn't need to happen. The goal is simple: get the door operating cleanly again so it latches the way it should and stays that way.

When you actually need this

Lock repair makes sense when the lock itself is still worth saving, but something in the hardware has started to fail in a specific, fixable way. A key that suddenly turns hard, a deadbolt that needs pressure on the door before it will throw, a knob or lever that feels loose, a latch that hangs up and won't spring back cleanly, or a cylinder that spins with too much play are all signs that a repair visit is the right first step. In a lot of homes, the problem is not "bad lock, replace it." It's wear in the latch, mounting screws that have backed out, a strike that drifted out of line, sag in the door, dried lubricant collecting grit inside the cylinder, or a tailpiece or internal return spring that isn't doing its job. On older front doors, storm doors, side entries, and garage-to-house doors, those small issues add up until the lock feels unreliable even though the main hardware can still be put back in proper working order.

This service is also the right answer when the lock was installed correctly once and then the door changed around it. Seasonal movement, settlement, heavy use, and repeated slamming can pull a deadbolt and strike out of alignment. That's common on wood doors and frames, and it shows up as scraping, sticking, or a bolt that only works when the door is pushed, lifted, or pulled. A repair call lets the locksmith diagnose the whole setup: cylinder, latch or bolt, strike, hinges, backset, and door fit. If the lock is a standard keyed knob, keyed lever, deadbolt, mortise cylinder, or jimmy-proof style that still has sound parts and a solid body, repair often restores normal operation without replacing hardware that doesn't need replacing. That matters when you want to keep matching trim or keep a lockset that fits the door well.

Sometimes, though, repair is not the best answer. If the lock has visible damage from force, a cracked housing, a bent bolt, missing internal parts, severe corrosion, or a worn keyway that no longer holds pins consistently, replacement is usually the cleaner long-term fix. The same goes for builder-grade hardware that has reached the end of its useful life, or for a front door that never had a proper deadbolt and really needs one installed instead of more work on a weak latchset. If your concern is key control rather than mechanical failure, rekeying is the better service. If you're changing hardware style, upgrading security, adding a keypad, or moving to a smart lock, repair can solve today's symptom but won't meet the real goal.

A good service call is about sorting those cases out honestly. If a mailbox lock is seized, that is usually a replacement job, not a repair. If a door lock near Pulaski Highway works but binds because the frame shifted, repair and adjustment may be all it needs. If a deadbolt on an older home in Elkton has a solid body and good cylinder but won't line up with the strike, replacing it alone won't cure the door problem. The point of lock repair is to fix the actual failure mode, preserve usable hardware when that makes sense, and recommend rekeying, replacement, deadbolt installation, or a higher-security upgrade when repair would only postpone the real problem.

How the work is done

Most lock repair starts with figuring out whether the trouble is in the lock itself, the door, or the way the hardware was installed. A sticking deadbolt can be caused by worn pins and springs inside the cylinder, but it can also come from a sagging door, a shifted strike plate, paint buildup, or screws that have loosened in the jamb. The first step is to check how the key turns, how the latch or bolt travels, whether the thumbturn binds, and whether the door has to be pushed or lifted to lock. On a front entry in Elkton, for example, a lock may seem bad when the real problem is seasonal movement in the door and frame. The outside trim, inside escutcheon, strike, latch faceplate, and mounting screws are all inspected before anything gets swapped out.

Once the cause is pinned down, the hardware is taken apart far enough to service the failed parts without replacing good ones. On a standard keyed knob, lever, or single-cylinder deadbolt, that can mean removing the cylinder, checking the plug for scoring, testing spring tension, and looking for pins that are mushroomed, dirty, or no longer lifting cleanly to the shear line. If the keyway is packed with grit, old lubricant, or metal dust from wear, the cylinder is cleaned and relubricated with the right product for that lock type. If the latch is the problem, the technician checks for a weak return spring, a bent tailpiece, or a latch bolt that is dragging because the chassis has shifted. With mortise hardware, there is more to inspect: the case, hub, spindle action, set screw, cam, and the fit of the mortise cylinder itself.

Door alignment work is often part of a proper repair. If the deadbolt hits low or high on the strike, the cure may be tightening hinge screws, replacing stripped screws with longer ones where appropriate, adjusting the strike location, or correcting a latch that was installed out of level. A loose lock body can also work against itself over time. Through-bolts back off, lever trim gets play, and repeated use enlarges screw holes in a wood door. Repair means restoring solid mounting so the cylinder and latch stay in line under normal use. In older homes near Chesapeake City, it is common to find layers of repainting around faceplates and strikes that change clearances just enough to make a lock feel rough.

After the mechanical work is done, the lock is tested through full cycles with the door open and closed to make sure it throws, retracts, and seats the way it should. That final check matters because some locks operate smoothly in the hand but bind under the pressure of a closed door. If repair will leave an unreliable result-such as a cracked housing, a worn plug beyond service limits, or badly chewed mounting points-the next step is explained plainly, whether that means rekeying related cylinders to match, replacing one component, or moving to a different grade of deadbolt. The goal is to keep sound hardware in service when it makes sense and correct the real cause of the problem rather than treating every sticky lock like a replacement job.

What it takes to do it properly

A proper lock repair starts with knowing whether the problem is in the lock itself, the door, the frame, or the way the hardware was installed. A deadbolt that binds can be suffering from worn pins, a bent tailpiece, a loose cylinder, paint buildup in the bolt pocket, hinge sag, or a strike that drifted out of line as the house settled. A knob or lever set that feels loose may have stripped through-bolts, a cracked chassis, or a latch that no longer retracts cleanly because the door edge is pinching it. To sort that out, the job needs more than a screwdriver. It takes the right drivers and bits, plug followers, pinning tools, calipers, lubrication that won't gum up the works, replacement tailpieces, strike shims, longer screws for solid anchoring, latch parts, and a working knowledge of common residential hardware from Schlage, Kwikset, Yale, Baldwin, and similar brands.

It also takes information. Before touching anything, you need to know what the lock is supposed to do, what it's doing instead, whether the problem is new or gradual, whether another contractor already adjusted the door, and whether the customer wants to keep the existing keying. On a house near Main Street in Elkton, for example, an older wood door may swell and shrink enough through the seasons to throw off a deadbolt that is mechanically fine. In that case, replacing the cylinder would miss the real issue. The same goes for smart locks: if the bolt is fighting the frame, the electronics may get blamed when the actual repair is mechanical alignment. Good work means reading wear marks on the strike, checking door reveal, testing latch and deadbolt travel with the door open and closed, and inspecting screw length, backset, handing, and bore prep before deciding what stays and what goes.

Judgement is the difference between a repair that lasts and a bodged one that only feels better for the moment. Some locks should be serviced, cleaned, tightened, and realigned. Some should be rekeyed while they are apart because the cylinder is sound but key control has changed. Some should be replaced because the case is cracked, the cam is worn out, the spring tension is gone, or the parts are no longer dependable. A proper result also means matching hardware to the door and the use. A lightweight interior latch does not belong on an exterior side entry. A long throw deadbolt needs a frame and strike that can accept it cleanly. If the door is out of plane, forcing the bolt into a bad opening is not repair; it's just adding wear.

What separates careful work from a shortcut is that the lock is tested as a system when the repair is done. The key should enter smoothly, turn without grinding, and withdraw without hanging up. The inside thumbturn or lever should operate without slop. The latch should catch without needing a shove, and the deadbolt should project fully into a strike that is actually centered. Screws should be snugged without stripping the door, trim should sit flat, and lubrication should be applied where it helps rather than sprayed everywhere. A bodged repair often hides the symptom with a blast of oil, a mis-sized screw, or a strike opened up too aggressively. A proper one leaves the door closing right, the lock operating cleanly, and the hardware still doing its job after regular daily use across homes from Cecil County to Kent County.

Getting it right the first time

A lock repair goes wrong when someone treats the symptom instead of the cause. A key that drags, a deadbolt that needs a hard push, or a knob that feels loose can come from very different problems. We see worn pins and springs inside the cylinder, but we also see doors that have dropped on the hinges, strike plates set too tight, screws pulling out of a wood jamb, and tailpieces that were cut or assembled wrong during an earlier install. If all you do is swap the cylinder because the key feels rough, the bolt may still bind every time the weather shifts. If all you do is shave the strike opening to make the bolt clear, you can leave the lock misaligned and under strain. Getting it right the first time means checking the whole door, the latch, the bolt throw, the cylinder, and the hardware that ties them together.

A lot of second visits come from mixing parts that almost fit. Residential hardware is full of small differences that matter: tubular latches, mortise cylinders, rim cylinders, single-cylinder deadbolts, interconnected entry sets, and smart lock retrofits all have their own tolerances. A Kwikset key-in-knob issue is not repaired the same way as a Schlage deadbolt with a worn bolt cam, and a Baldwin handleset with a loose through-bolt problem is a different animal again. We look for spindle wear, stripped set screws, cracked chassis springs, sloppy latch retraction, and cylinders that have developed play from use. We also check whether the screw lengths are correct. Hardware that is cinched down too hard can bind; hardware left loose will shift and work itself out of alignment.

Another common mistake is repairing only what is visible at the edge of the door. The lock may be the part that fails in your hand, but the trouble can be at the jamb or in the door prep itself. Paint buildup inside the strike, a warped slab, hinge screws no longer biting, or a mislocated bore from a past replacement can all create repeat problems. On older homes, we sometimes find a deadbolt installed above a spring latch with the spacing slightly off, so the trim fights for room and the inside turn piece never sits square. In newer setups, smart lock conversions can leave the tailpiece preloaded or the bolt handing wrong, which causes intermittent sticking that gets blamed on the batteries or app when the real issue is mechanical.

Avoiding a second visit comes down to diagnosis, fit, and testing under real operating conditions. We check how the lock behaves with the door open and with it fully closed, because some faults only show up when the latch and bolt meet the strike. We verify that the key turns cleanly, the bolt extends fully, the thumbturn has consistent resistance, and the door closes without having to lift, pull, or lean on it. If repair is the right answer, we repair the existing hardware and correct the alignment so the lock is not forced to do the door's job. If a part is too far gone, we explain where repair stops making sense and what replacement would solve that repair cannot. That approach is how we avoid papering over the issue and leaving you with the same callback a short while later.

What to have ready

Before you call about a lock that sticks, sags, or won't turn cleanly, it helps to gather a few basic details from the door and the hardware. Look at whether the problem is on a front entry, side door, storm door, interior privacy lock, patio handle set, or a mailbox. If you can do it safely, note the lock type: keyed knob, lever lock, single-cylinder deadbolt, double-cylinder deadbolt, mortise lock, or a smart lock with a key override. Check whether the key still goes in fully, whether it turns partway and binds, whether the thumbturn feels loose, or whether the bolt drags against the strike plate. Also pay attention to when it acts up. Some locks work fine in dry weather and bind when humidity swells the door or the frame shifts. Others only fail when the door has to be pushed hard to latch, which points to alignment rather than a bad cylinder.

It also helps to have the key that normally operates the lock, along with any spare that behaves differently. A worn key can mimic lock failure, while a rough new key can reveal burrs or pin wear inside the cylinder. If the lock has brand markings, a photo of the inside and outside trim can be useful, especially for Kwikset, Schlage, Yale, Baldwin, or a keypad deadbolt where the trim and bolt have to be matched correctly. If anything has already been tried, say so plainly. Dry lubricant, oil, tightening the mounting screws, a recent paint job, or a door that was forced shut all change the diagnosis. If the lock is on a rental, condo, or shared mailbox, have any approval you need lined up so the repair doesn't stall once the issue is identified.

On the day of service, the main thing is clear access to the door and a quick explanation of what the lock has been doing. Move pets away from the work area, turn on porch or hallway lights if the entry is dim, and make sure someone with authority to approve the work is available. The technician will usually start by checking door fit, hinge condition, latch and deadbolt travel, strike location, screw tightness, and cylinder function before recommending repair or replacement. A lot of residential lock problems come from sagging hinges, a mispositioned strike, loose through-bolts, a tailpiece that isn't engaging cleanly, or a bolt rubbing because the door is dropping in the frame. In those cases, replacing the whole lock can be unnecessary.

If replacement parts are needed, the decision often comes down to condition and compatibility, not guesswork. A lock body with a cracked housing, a deadbolt with a bent bolt assembly, or a badly worn cylinder may not be worth rebuilding. On the other hand, a good quality deadbolt that's simply out of alignment can often be corrected and kept in service. If you want the repaired lock to match the rest of the house, mention that early, especially if other doors are already on one key. That's also the right time to ask whether rekeying, a deadbolt upgrade, or a smart lock makes sense after the repair. For homes around Elkton or near US 40, the most useful calls are the ones where the symptoms are described clearly and the door can be tested under the same conditions that cause the trouble.

Frequently asked questions

If a lock only sticks when the weather changes, is repair still worth doing?

Yes. Seasonal sticking usually points to a door alignment issue, swelling at the frame, or a cylinder that is starting to bind. If you leave it alone, people tend to force the key or push harder on the door, which wears parts faster and can lead to a lockout. A repair can include adjusting the strike, checking the latch travel, and cleaning or servicing the cylinder so the lock works smoothly again.

Can you repair the lock if my key turns, but the deadbolt doesn't fully extend?

Often, yes. That symptom can come from worn internal parts, a loose tailpiece, a misaligned strike, or a deadbolt rubbing because the door has shifted. The lock itself may not be the only problem. The right fix depends on whether the key and cylinder are operating normally and whether the bolt moves freely with the door open. In some cases repair makes sense; in others, replacement is the better call.

Is it a good idea to spray lubricant into a residential lock that feels rough?

Not always. The wrong product can attract dirt, gum up pins, or wash debris deeper into the cylinder. If the key is hard to insert or turn, the cause may be wear, damage to the key, or a door alignment problem rather than a dry lock. Light lock-specific service can help, but soaking it with general-purpose spray usually does not fix the root issue and can make later repair more difficult.

If the lock is loose on the door, does tightening the screws solve it?

Sometimes, but a loose lock often means more than backed-out screws. The mounting holes may be worn, the door face may be compressed, or the lock chassis may have shifted enough to affect latch or deadbolt alignment. Simply tightening everything can leave the lock under tension and still hard to use. A proper repair checks the hardware, the door prep, and the strike so the lock is secure and operates without strain.
