Deadbolt Installation

A grade-rated deadbolt fitted properly, with the strike and strike screws that actually make it hold. Available across Northeast Maryland & Delaware.

Call (443) 545-2775 Request service

If your front door has a loose lock, a short screw in the strike, or a deadbolt that doesn't throw cleanly, that's the kind of problem we see in homes across Cecil County and the surrounding area. A lot of the calls come from homeowners who want the door to feel solid again, not just look locked. We install deadbolts the right way, with the lock, strike plate, and strike screws matched up so the bolt has something real to hold against.

We handle residential deadbolt installation across Northeast Maryland and Delaware, and most of the work is done at your home. That means we can fit the hardware to the door you already have, check the alignment, and make sure the latch and bolt aren't fighting the frame. If the door has been repaired before, painted over, or drilled for the wrong hardware, we can work with that too. On a lot of older homes near Elkton, the issue isn't the deadbolt itself, it's the way the door and frame have worn over time. We take care of that part so the lock does its job when you need it to.

When you actually need this

Deadbolt installation makes sense when the door either doesn't have a true deadbolt yet or has one that was never fitted in a way that gives it much to work with. A keyed knob lock by itself is not the same thing. The spring latch in a knob or lever can secure the door for ordinary use, but it is not built for the same job as a grade-rated deadbolt with a solid throw and a properly anchored strike. This service also comes up after a move, after a breakup, after a lost key, or any time you no longer trust who may still have access. In those cases, the question is not just whether there is a lock on the door, but whether the main entry points have a deadbolt that seats cleanly, throws fully, and is tied into the frame with screws and hardware that actually belong there.

It's also the right call when the existing deadbolt is the wrong fit for the door. That happens more than people think. The backset may be wrong, the bore hole may be rough or oversized, the edge bore may not line up, or the strike may be set where the bolt barely catches. On older wood doors, the bolt can scrape because the slab has shifted with the seasons. On some steel or fiberglass doors, the lock body may be acceptable but the strike area in the jamb is weak or poorly aligned. Installing a new deadbolt is not just swapping the trim you can see from the outside. The real value is in matching the lock to the door thickness, latch type, handing, and frame condition, then correcting the strike so the bolt enters without binding. If the goal is better control of keys, this is often paired with rekeying so the new hardware works on the keying plan you want.

But there are plenty of situations where deadbolt installation is not the best answer. If you already have a solid deadbolt and the problem is simply that old keys are still out in the world, rekeying is usually the cleaner fix. If the bolt works but sticks, drags, or only turns with the door pushed hard against the frame, that points to adjustment or door lock repair before replacement. If the lock body is decent and the issue is a worn tailpiece, loose cylinder, bent strike, or screws backing out, a targeted repair may restore normal operation without changing the whole setup. And if the door itself is split, the jamb is crushed, or the frame near the strike is failing, no new deadbolt will perform the way it should until the surrounding material is dealt with.

There are also cases where another type of installation is a better fit. A side door that sees constant family traffic may be a good candidate for a smart lock with an integrated deadbolt, especially if key control has become a headache. A house with one front cylinder and several mismatched keys may need lock replacement across the entries so everything can be keyed alike. If the concern is package theft or a tenant mailbox that no longer secures, mailbox lock replacement solves a different problem entirely. And when someone asks for "the strongest deadbolt possible," the answer may be a high-security lock installation rather than a basic residential model, particularly if the cylinder, key control, and resistance to forced rotation matter as much as the bolt itself. The point is to match the service to the problem, not force every door into the same answer.

How the work is done

A proper deadbolt installation starts before any holes are drilled. The first step is checking the door itself: wood, steel, or fiberglass; slab thickness; backset; edge bore size; and whether there's already a lock above the knob or lever. The jamb matters just as much. If the frame is split, soft, out of square, or shimmed poorly, a good lock won't do what it's supposed to do. The fit of the door is checked next. If it drags, has a wide reveal on one side, or moves in the opening when it closes, that affects where the bolt should land. For exterior doors, the installer also looks at weatherstripping, thresholds, and any seasonal swelling that can make a bolt bind. Then the right hardware is chosen for the door and the use: single-cylinder for most front doors, double-cylinder only where code and life-safety concerns are addressed, and grade-rated hardware where strength and service life matter.

Once the layout is confirmed, the cross-bore and edge bore are measured or corrected so the deadbolt sits level and the tailpiece lines up cleanly with the interior thumbturn. On a fresh install, the bore spacing has to be exact so the deadbolt doesn't crowd the knob set or weaken the stile. The latch face is mortised flush into the door edge, and the bolt is set so it throws and retracts without scraping. If the door is metal-clad or has a reinforced edge, the prep has to match the manufacturer's template. The cylinder and trim are installed square, with through-bolts tightened enough to secure the lock but not so much that the chassis binds. On better hardware, that difference shows up right away in how smoothly the key turns and how positively the thumbturn feels.

The strike side is where a lot of installations are won or lost. A deadbolt is only as good as the strike it seats into. The jamb is marked from the actual bolt position, not just from a paper template, because many doors close slightly high, low, or tight to one side. The strike pocket is cut deep enough for full bolt throw, and the strike is set flat so the bolt enters cleanly instead of riding an edge. Short factory screws often get replaced with longer structural screws that reach solid framing, which helps the load transfer past the trim and thin jamb material. If the reveal is uneven or the door has settled, the strike may need to be shifted or reinforced so the bolt doesn't hit, bounce, or stop short. That's the difference between a deadbolt that technically locks and one that actually holds.

After the hardware is in, the lock is tested through repeated cycles with the door open and closed. The key should enter fully, turn without excessive resistance, and come out only in the proper position for that model. The thumbturn should move the bolt completely without forcing it. Any rub points are corrected by adjusting the strike, refining the mortise, or addressing door alignment. If the customer wants matching keys, that's where rekeying can be done to match existing house keys or separate the new deadbolt onto its own keyway. On some doors, a standard cylindrical deadbolt is the best fit; on others, a high-security deadbolt or a smart deadbolt makes more sense, but only if the door prep, handing, and latch alignment support it. In homes from Elkton to Bel Air, the goal is the same every time: a lock that closes cleanly, throws fully, and isn't relying on luck or oversized holes to work.

What it takes to do it properly

A proper deadbolt install starts with more than a drill and a box from the hardware shelf. The lock has to match the door, the frame, and the way the house is actually used. That means checking door thickness, backset, bore size, edge prep, handing, jamb condition, and whether the frame is wood, steel, or wrapped over older lumber. On an exterior door, the deadbolt should be grade-rated, with a solid bolt throw and a strike that belongs to that lock, not a random plate left over from an earlier job. The tools matter too: sharp hole saws, the right jigs, chisels that cut clean mortises, driver bits that don't cam out screws, and a way to verify alignment so the bolt enters the strike without scraping or binding.

The information side of the job is where a lot of bad installs start to show. A locksmith has to read the door before choosing hardware. A wood door with seasonal movement may need different adjustment than a steel entry door. A decorative glass-panel door may limit placement. Some homes need a single-cylinder deadbolt, while others are better served by a double-cylinder setup because of nearby glass, though that choice has life-safety implications and needs to be discussed clearly. If the customer wants smart access, the deadbolt has to work with the door's prep, the latch alignment, and the user's habits. A keypad lock that binds on the frame will eat batteries and frustrate the owner no matter how nice the features look on paper.

Judgement is what separates a secure result from a bodged one. The deadbolt itself is only part of the assembly. If the strike is set shallow, attached with short screws, or mortised into split jamb wood, the bolt may turn but the door still won't hold up the way it should. A proper install often includes reinforcing the strike area, correcting a sagging door, and making sure the weatherstrip isn't forcing the latch side out of line. On older homes around Elkton, it's common to find doors that have been adjusted around paint buildup, settlement, or repeated hardware swaps. If those issues aren't corrected, the new deadbolt inherits all of them.

You can usually spot a rushed job by the symptoms: chipped finish around the bore, a crooked faceplate, a bolt that needs pressure on the door to lock, loose trim, mismatched screws, or a strike plate that was moved just enough to make the key turn. A proper result feels different. The key turns cleanly, the thumbturn has a solid stop, the bolt centers in the strike, and the door closes without a shove. That kind of result takes measuring, fitting, and knowing when to repair the surrounding hardware instead of pretending the deadbolt alone will fix everything. It's the difference between hanging a lock on a door and installing a deadbolt system that actually works on a house near US 40 in real daily use.

Getting it right the first time

A deadbolt can be a solid upgrade, but a lot of problems start with installs that look fine from the hallway and fail where it matters. The most common mistake is treating the lock as a stand-alone part instead of part of the whole door system. If the bore hole is slightly off, the bolt drags. If the cross-bore is too loose, the lock body shifts over time and the trim starts binding. If the door is sagging on worn hinges, a brand-new deadbolt still won't throw cleanly into the strike. Another frequent issue is mixing parts from different locksets, especially when someone tries to reuse an old strike or latch because it "almost fits." With deadbolts, almost is where callbacks come from.

Getting it right means checking door thickness, backset, edge prep, latch faceplate size, and how the door actually closes under normal pressure. A proper install also means using a grade-rated deadbolt that matches the use of the door. A front entry that sees heavy daily traffic has different demands than a side door or a door from the house into the garage. Single-cylinder and double-cylinder deadbolts each have their place, but the choice should be made with the door layout, glass nearby, and life-safety concerns in mind. The strike matters just as much as the cylinder. A light-gauge strike with short screws may hold the trim in place, but it does very little for the door frame. When the strike is upgraded and anchored correctly into solid framing, the deadbolt has something real to work against.

A second visit is often caused by issues that weren't addressed during the first one. Weatherstripping can push the door out of alignment. A warped slab can make the bolt hit the edge of the strike opening instead of entering cleanly. On older homes, the jamb may have been patched or painted so many times that the strike sits proud and the door never closes right. If the lock is keyed alike with existing hardware, that has to be confirmed before drilling starts, not after the new deadbolt is already on the door. Finish matters too. Satin nickel, matte black, polished brass, and aged bronze don't wear the same way or match every existing knobset, and a mismatched replacement often leads to another service call when the homeowner decides to swap the rest.

The goal is a deadbolt that locks smoothly without lifting the door, forcing the key, or slamming the frame. That takes more than putting a new cylinder on the door. It means correcting alignment, choosing the right latch and strike, setting screw length properly, and checking operation with the door open and closed. It also means being honest about when a deadbolt install should really be paired with lock replacement, door lock repair, or a smart lock installation so the hardware works together instead of fighting itself. That kind of attention is what keeps a clean install from turning into a repeat problem after a week of normal use.

What to have ready

Before you call, it helps to look at the door and the lock you already have, even if you're not sure what you're seeing. A clear photo of the inside trim, the outside cylinder, the edge of the door, and the strike on the frame tells a lot. If there's already a deadbolt, check whether it's a single-cylinder deadbolt with a key outside and thumbturn inside, or a double-cylinder deadbolt that needs a key on both sides. Also note whether the door is wood, steel, or fiberglass, and whether it swings cleanly or rubs, sags, or needs a shove to latch. A good deadbolt depends on door fit as much as the lock itself. If you know the backset, bore hole size, or crossbore size, that's useful, but most people don't, and that's fine.

It also helps to decide what problem you're trying to solve. Some people want a basic grade-rated deadbolt added to a front door that only has a knob lock. Others are replacing a loose builder-grade lock, fixing a deadbolt that binds because the bolt is hitting the strike wrong, or matching a new deadbolt to existing house keys through rekeying. If you have concerns about a side door, a door between the house and garage, or a rental turnover, say that up front. If there's a storm door, thick decorative trim, sidelights, or glass close to the lock area, mention it. Those details affect what hardware makes sense and whether a double-cylinder setup raises life-safety concerns.

On the day, the first step is usually a close look at the door, frame, alignment, and existing prep. That means checking whether the bore holes are clean and correctly placed, whether the latch edge is chewed up, whether the jamb is solid, and whether the strike area can take longer screws that anchor into framing instead of just soft trim. A proper install is not just dropping in new hardware. The bolt has to throw fully without scraping, the strike has to line up so the door isn't held under pressure, and the screws and reinforcements have to support the rating of the deadbolt. If the frame is split, the door is warped, or the old holes are oversized, that gets addressed before the new lock is set.

Once the hardware choice is confirmed, the lock is fitted, adjusted, and tested from both sides with the door open and closed. If you asked for keyed alike service, smart lock installation, or a lock replacement that matches other doors, that can be handled as part of the visit if the door and hardware allow it. You should expect a straightforward walk-through at the end: how the thumbturn feels, how far the bolt should extend, whether the key should be inserted upright or reversed depending on the keyway, and what normal wear to watch for over time. If your home is near Route 40 or closer to Bel Air, the process is the same: the goal is a deadbolt that seats cleanly, locks smoothly, and actually holds because the door and frame were treated as part of the job, not an afterthought.

Frequently asked questions

Can you add a deadbolt to a door that doesn't already have one, or does the whole door need to be replaced?

Usually a deadbolt can be added to an existing door if the door and frame are in decent shape and there's enough room above the knob or lever for proper spacing. Wood, fiberglass, and many steel doors can all take a new deadbolt. What matters most is the condition of the edge, jamb, and strike area. If the door is split, badly warped, or the frame is weak, the opening may need repair before a deadbolt should be installed.

If my door is hard to close or the latch doesn't line up well now, should I wait on a deadbolt install?

It's better to deal with the alignment problem first, or at least at the same visit. A deadbolt works best when the door closes squarely and the bolt enters the strike without rubbing or binding. If the door is sagging, the weatherstrip is forcing it out of position, or the frame has shifted, a new lock alone won't fix that. In some cases the strike can be adjusted, but sometimes the jamb or hinges need attention too.

Can a new deadbolt be keyed to match my existing house key?

Often yes, but it depends on the brand, keyway, and condition of the other locks. If your current knob, lever, or handle set uses a compatible keyway, the new deadbolt can often be pinned to match it. If the existing hardware is worn out, uncommon, or from a different key family, matching may not make sense. In that case, replacing or rekeying the related locks is usually the cleaner solution.

Is it a good idea to put a smart deadbolt on the main entry, or is a standard keyed deadbolt still better?

Either can be the right choice, but the door has to be solid and the install has to be precise. A smart deadbolt can be convenient for codes, access control, and not carrying a key. A standard keyed deadbolt is simpler and has fewer electronic parts to manage. The better choice depends on who uses the door, whether you want app features, and how exposed the opening is to weather. Not every smart model is a good fit for every door.
