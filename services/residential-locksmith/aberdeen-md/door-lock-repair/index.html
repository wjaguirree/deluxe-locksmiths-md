Why Door Locks Fail

We see a lot of the same shortcuts on door hardware in Aberdeen homes, and most of them start with trying to make a sticking lock work without fixing what caused it. A loose strike plate gets pushed back into the jamb instead of being reset. A deadbolt that rubs gets forced harder each night until the screws strip or the bolt starts dragging the frame. A latch that only catches if the door is lifted gets blamed on the lock, when the door has actually settled and needs alignment. Around Beards Hill and the older streets near US-40, that kind of wear shows up fast in doors that have been repaired before, painted over, or patched after a move.

The consequence is usually the same: the door stops closing cleanly, keys start turning roughly, and people begin slamming the door to get it shut. That puts stress on the latch, knob, deadbolt, and hinges all at once. In worse cases, the bolt never fully engages, so the door looks shut but isn't secure. We also see homeowners swap parts one at a time, trying a new knob on an old misaligned door or replacing a lock when the real issue is sagging hardware. That wastes good parts and leaves the underlying problem in place.

Our approach is to find what's out of line and correct it at the door, not just cover up the symptom. When the hardware is realigned and repaired properly, the latch catches the way it should, the door closes without force, and the lock stops taking the abuse that wears it out early.

On the phone, people usually want to know whether the problem is the lock or the door itself. The honest answer is that it can be either, and sometimes both. If the latch only catches when you lift or pull the door, if the deadbolt feels tight, or if the knob turns but nothing lines up, we can usually narrow it down fast enough to know what repair is needed.

Another common question is whether they need to replace the whole lock. Not always. A lot of the time we can repair the strike, re-seat the hardware, or fix the alignment and keep the existing lock in place. If the mechanism is broken inside, rusted through, or too worn from repeated use, replacement may be the better call. We'll explain the difference clearly so you can decide what makes sense for the door.

Door Lock Repair in Aberdeen - common questions

My front door in Aberdeen shuts, but the latch only catches if I push hard. Can that be repaired?

Usually, yes. That symptom often points to a door that's out of alignment, a loose strike, or a worn latch. We check how the door meets the frame, then repair the part that's keeping the latch from seating correctly. If the lock body itself is worn out, we'll let you know that too.

What should I have ready before you come out for a door lock repair?

It helps to know which door is acting up, whether it's the knob, deadbolt, or both, and what the door has been doing lately. If you can leave the area around the door clear, that makes the work easier. If there's a key that still turns, keep that handy so we can test the lock after repair.

I'm worried a repair won't hold and the door will still be easy to force open. Is that a real risk?

It can be if the problem is only patched instead of corrected. A loose strike or a sagging door can leave the latch weak even if the lock turns normally. We focus on the alignment, the hardware, and the latch engagement so the door closes and locks the way it should.

Should I repair the existing lock or replace it on my Aberdeen rental property?

If the lock is solid but the latch isn't lining up, repair is often the better choice. If the inside parts are worn, bent, or damaged from repeated use, replacement may make more sense. We look at the condition of the door, frame, and lock together so you're not replacing parts that still have good service left in them.

Can you repair a lock on an older house door that swells when the weather changes?

Yes, in many cases. Older doors often need more than a simple lock tweak because the wood moves and the frame shifts with the seasons. We can adjust the strike, correct the fit, and repair worn hardware so the lock works more reliably when the door changes shape a bit.
