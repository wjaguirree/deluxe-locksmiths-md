Before You Touch the Lock

If a key snaps off, the best first step is to stop turning the lock. Extra force can drive the blade deeper, bend the plug, or damage the pins inside. If part of the key is still sticking out, you can take a clear look and tell us whether the break is flush, broken off a little inside, or jammed in a mortise or rim lock. That matters in New Castle, especially in the Historic District where older doors and original hardware often don't behave like newer locks on Route 13. If the break happened at a gate, mailbox, storefront, or house door, have the lock type and the key brand handy if you know them. Those small details help us bring the right tools and decide whether we should extract the blade on site or deal with worn hardware at the same time.

What to leave alone is just as important. Don't push the fragment with a knife, paperclip, drill bit, or glue. Don't spray random oils into the keyway, and don't keep trying a second key if the first one snapped because of resistance. On older doors around Delaware Street, where settling and original locks are part of the building, forcing the mechanism can turn a key problem into a damaged lock case or a door that won't line up. We can work with the stuck fragment, remove it without rough handling, and check whether the key failed because of wear, a misaligned lock, or a bent key blank. If you can tell us what the lock is doing and leave the hardware untouched, we've got a much better shot at getting you back to normal without extra damage.

There are a few common versions of this job. In the simplest one, the key snapped while turning, and part of the blade is still sticking out. We can usually grip it and pull it without disturbing the lock. If the break is deeper in the cylinder, we use extraction tools to reach it and clear the passage.

A second version is a key that broke because the lock was already hard to turn. In that case, extraction is only part of the work. We check whether the plug is binding, whether the key is worn, or whether the door is out of alignment. A third version shows up when an older lock has fragile internal parts. Then the job has to be done more carefully so we don't turn a key break into a lock replacement.

Broken Key Extraction in New Castle - common questions

How does broken key extraction work on an old New Castle house lock?

We inspect the lock first, because older mortise and rim locks can be more delicate than newer hardware. If the broken blade can be reached, we remove it with the right tools and test the lock after. If the cylinder is worn or binding, we'll point that out before you try to force anything.

What should I have ready before you come out for a broken key in New Castle?

Have the door open or closed as it is, and keep any remaining key pieces if you still have them. It also helps to know whether the lock has been sticking or if the key snapped during normal use. That tells us whether this is just an extraction or a bigger lock issue.

Can forcing the broken piece make the lock worse?

Yes. Pushing on the broken blade can drive it deeper or damage the cylinder pins. If the lock is older or already tight, that risk goes up. It's better to leave it alone and let us remove it cleanly so we can check whether the lock still turns the way it should.

If my key broke, do I need a new lock or just a new key?

Not always a new lock. If the cylinder still works and the break was caused by a worn key, a fresh key may be enough. If the lock is bent, gritty, or hard to turn, we may recommend repair or replacement. We only suggest what fits the actual condition of the hardware.

What if the key broke off inside a deadbolt on Delaware Street and nothing is sticking out?

That can still be handled. When no blade is exposed, we use extraction tools to reach the break inside the cylinder. If the lock is a fragile older style or the fragment is jammed hard, removal may take extra care. We'll tell you if the lock needs repair after the piece comes out.
