Broken Key Extraction in Conowingo - common questions

How do you tell if my key is broken off in the lock or if the lock itself is jammed?

If the key won't turn and part of it is still visible, that often points to a broken blade or a bent key. If there's no key visible, the lock may still be holding a snapped piece inside. A jammed lock usually feels gritty or stiff before the key fails. We check the cylinder first so we don't force the wrong thing.

What should I have ready before you come out to Conowingo for a broken key?

Have the exact lock location in mind, such as the vehicle door, ignition, deadbolt, or padlock, and keep any spare key you do have nearby. If it's a car, know the make, model, and year. If it's a house lock, tell us whether the key broke while turning, inserting, or removing. That helps us bring the right tools.

Can a broken key damage the lock if I keep trying to turn it?

Yes. More turning can push the broken piece deeper, bend the cylinder parts, or leave burrs that make the lock harder to use later. If the key is snapped, stop using it. A steady extraction is safer than forcing the remains and turning a repairable lock into a replacement job.

If the key snapped in my car door at the dam, do you always need to replace the lock?

Not always. If the cylinder is still sound and the broken blade comes out cleanly, the lock may keep working. If the key broke because the lock is worn, corroded, or sticky, the cylinder may need service or replacement. The decision depends on what the lock looks and feels like after the broken piece is removed.

What if the key broke off flush and I can't grab it with pliers?

That's common, and pliers usually won't help once the break is flush with the face of the lock. The safer move is a proper extraction tool and a careful pull from the cylinder. If the break is deep, we may need to work the lock from the front rather than trying to hook the blade from the edge.
