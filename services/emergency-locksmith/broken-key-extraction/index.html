Broken Key Extraction

Key snapped off in the lock? We extract the blade and get you a key that works again. Available across Northeast Maryland & Delaware.

Call (443) 545-2775 Request service

A snapped key usually turns up when somebody's trying to get in after work, lock up a shop for the night, or leave in a hurry and the key gives way in the cylinder. It happens in house doors, padlocks, storefront locks, and vehicle locks, and once the broken blade is stuck in place, the lock may not turn or the rest of the key may not go in. We handle that kind of call across Cecil County and the surrounding area, mostly on site where the lock is installed.

The goal is to get the broken piece out without making the lock worse. That means the right pick, extractor, and a steady hand, not forcing the cylinder or drilling right away. If the key has split off cleanly, we can usually remove the blade, check the lock for damage, and cut a replacement key that matches what the lock still needs. If the lock is worn or the break left internal damage, we'll say so and talk through the next step before anything else is done.

For homes, rentals, commercial doors, and vehicles in and around Elkton, the problem is often a worn key, a dirty lock, or a key that was already bent before it snapped. If the broken piece is still visible, don't push it farther in or keep trying to turn it. That usually makes extraction harder.

When you actually need this

Broken key extraction is the right call when part of the key is still lodged in the lock and the lock itself may still be usable. That happens a lot with worn house keys, older brass copies, and car keys that have been bent on a crowded key ring for too long. A common example is the key turning hard in a deadbolt, then snapping at the shoulder so the blade stays in the cylinder. Another is a vehicle ignition where the key has already been cracked and finally separates during a start attempt. In those cases, the immediate job is to remove the broken piece without damaging the cylinder, then check whether the lock, ignition, or door hardware was the reason the key failed in the first place.

You also need this service when the broken piece is preventing normal use of the lock, even if you still have a spare. A spare key does not help if the original fragment is blocking the keyway. That comes up with Schlage and Kwikset residential cylinders, storefront mortise cylinders, padlocks, mailbox locks, and car door locks. On vehicles, the situation can be more involved because the key may have a transponder head, a laser-cut blade, or signs of wear that caused poor alignment long before the break. On a front door, repeated forcing can push the fragment deeper or scar the pins and warding. On an office door, the real issue may be a misaligned latch or a stiff mortise lock body that keeps eating keys until the hardware is corrected.

Just as important is knowing when broken key extraction is not the main service. If the key did not break but the door simply closed behind you, that is a lockout call, not an extraction. If all keys are missing, lost car key replacement or rekeying may be the better answer. If the cylinder turns but the bolt will not throw, the problem may be a failed deadbolt, a sagging door, or panic hardware that needs repair rather than extraction. If a safe key breaks, safe opening service may be required because the lock design, relockers, and door construction are a different category of work altogether.

There are also times when removing the fragment is only step one and replacing parts makes more sense than trying to save everything. If the key broke because the cylinder is badly worn, packed with debris, corroded from weather, or damaged after someone forced it, extraction alone will not give you a reliable result. The same goes for an ignition with heavy wear, or a commercial lever set that has started binding under daily use. Around Elkton and along Route 40, a lot of service calls come from doors and vehicles that gave warning signs first: sticking, grinding, partial turns, or a key that only works when jiggled. When those signs are present, the better answer may be extraction plus a fresh key, rekeying, cylinder replacement, or hardware repair so the same break does not happen again.

How the work is done

Most broken key calls start with figuring out exactly what failed before anything gets pulled apart. A key can snap because the blade was already cracked at a cut, because the lock was dry and binding, because the wrong key was forced, or because the cylinder itself has worn pins or a tired plug. The first step is to identify the lock type and where the break happened. A house deadbolt, a storefront mortise cylinder, an ignition, and a vehicle door lock all fail in different ways and need different handling. The remaining piece might be flush with the face of the cylinder, slightly proud of it, or buried deeper past the keyway entrance. The condition of the lock matters just as much as the fragment. If the plug is under tension, if a wafer is hanging up, or if debris is packed into the keyway, forcing an extraction can scar the warding or bend internal parts that were still usable.

From there, the work is careful access and controlled removal. The lock is stabilized so the cylinder housing does not twist while the fragment is being worked loose. On many residential and commercial cylinders, the locksmith will inspect the keyway with light and magnification, confirm the blade orientation, and check whether part of the shoulder is still visible. Lubrication may be used, but not as a cure-all; too much can carry grit farther into the plug. The extraction itself is done with purpose-made tools sized to the keyway, and the goal is to catch the broken blade without dragging against pins, wafers, or sidebars. In some cases the cylinder should be removed from the door for bench work because access at the opening is too cramped or because the face cap, trim ring, or tailpiece is interfering with a clean pull. On a vehicle, especially around newer ignitions, protecting the column shroud and avoiding damage to delicate internal components is part of the job, not an extra step.

Once the piece is out, the job is only half done. The lock still has to be tested to see whether the break was the cause or a symptom. A good service call checks plug rotation, pin or wafer movement, spring response, and visible wear on the keyway. If the lock is rough, the old key pattern may be read from the extracted blade and compared against the cuts on the customer's working copy, if there is one. That helps determine whether the key was badly worn, poorly duplicated, or bent from use. For automotive work, a snapped mechanical blade may be only one part of the problem if the head contains a transponder or remote that also needs to match the vehicle. For a house or business cylinder, the locksmith may advise whether the lock can stay in service, should be rekeyed, or should be replaced because the internal wear is what caused the key to fail in the first place.

The final step is making sure the customer leaves with a key that works cleanly and a lock that operates the way it should. That may mean cutting a fresh mechanical key to the proper code or by decoding the lock, not copying a worn key that would repeat the same trouble. It also means checking operation with the door open first so alignment issues do not get mistaken for a key problem. On older hardware around Elkton, or on commercial doors off US 40 that see heavy daily use, it is common to find more than one cause at once: a tired cylinder, a door out of alignment, and a key that has been copied too many generations away from the original. Good extraction work deals with the broken piece, but it also addresses the reason it got stuck there.

What it takes to do it properly

A proper broken key extraction starts with knowing exactly what failed. A key can snap because the blade metal is tired, because the cuts are badly worn from years of copying copies, because the lock is dry and dragging, or because the plug was under side pressure when someone forced the turn. That matters. A house deadbolt with a standard pin-tumbler cylinder calls for a different approach than a wafer-style vehicle door lock or an ignition cylinder with tighter tolerances. Before any tool comes out, the job needs basic facts: where the break happened, whether part of the key is still in hand, whether there's a working spare, and whether the lock was already acting up before the break. On a car, make, model, year, and whether the key has a chip are part of the picture, because extracting the blade is only half the job if the customer still needs a usable programmed key.

The equipment is more specialized than people think. A clean extraction may involve a lighted scope, a keyway reader, fine extraction picks sized to the profile, slim forceps, lubricant suited to locks, plug followers for certain cylinder work, and code-cutting equipment to produce a fresh key once the fragment is out. Sometimes the right move is to remove the cylinder from the door or hardware and service it on the bench rather than fight it in place. A locksmith also needs the blanks and machines to cut the right key type afterward, whether that's a basic residential key, a high-security sidewinder-style automotive key, or a commercial keyway with tighter warding. On jobs around Elkton and along Route 40, the difference between carrying general tools and carrying the right tools shows up fast.

Judgment is what keeps the job from turning into a bigger repair. If the broken piece is visible, that doesn't mean it should be yanked at once. Pulling at the wrong angle can burr the keyway, drag pins, or wedge the fragment deeper. If the lock is turned off center, or if there's tension trapped in the plug, that has to be addressed first or the extraction can damage the cylinder. If the customer only has a badly worn remaining half of the key, duplicating that wear usually leads to another failure. In those cases, a proper result often means decoding the lock or cutting to factory specifications instead of tracing a damaged sample.

What separates a clean result from a bodged one is what happens after the fragment comes out. A rushed job stops there. A professional checks whether the lock itself caused the break: sticky pins, worn wafers, debris, a weak return in an ignition, or a cylinder that's loose in the hardware. The key that replaces it should operate smoothly without excess force, and the lock should be tested through full travel, not just enough to prove it moves. On older hardware or cylinders exposed to weather near the Susquehanna, that extra check matters. The goal isn't just to remove metal from a keyway. It's to leave the customer with a lock and a key that work together the way they should.

Getting it right the first time

A broken key extraction goes wrong when the job gets treated like one simple pull. The broken blade is only part of the problem. On house locks, the break often starts with a worn brass key, a sticky pin-tumbler cylinder, or a deadbolt that has to be lifted or pushed to line up before it turns. On cars, the issue may be a tired key blade, a worn wafer lock, or an ignition that has developed rough spots. A lot of second visits happen because someone removes the broken piece, hands over a fresh copy made from the same worn key, and leaves the original cause untouched. That new key may work for a short time, then start hanging up, flexing, or shaving brass again.

The common mistakes are pretty consistent. People try tweezers, picks, or glue, and the fragment gets pushed deeper into the keyway. Lubricant gets sprayed in heavily and turns old dirt into sludge. A copy is made from a key that was already rounded off at the cuts and shoulder, so the replacement carries the same bad geometry into the lock. On commercial doors, a tight mortise cylinder or rim cylinder may be blamed on the key when the real problem is door alignment, latch pressure, or a cam that is no longer indexing cleanly. On vehicles, one lock may be more worn than another, so a key that turns the door can still bind in the ignition. If that difference isn't checked, the customer ends up with a "new" key that only solves half the problem.

Getting it right means inspecting the lock after the fragment is out, not just celebrating that the keyway is clear. The cylinder should be checked for scoring, bent warding, sticky pins or wafers, and signs that a broken tip has been dragging inside. The key itself matters just as much. If the only sample is badly worn, a proper replacement may need to be generated from code, from lock reading, or from manufacturer specifications rather than traced from a tired duplicate. For cars with a chip key or remote head key, the blade cut and the electronic programming both have to match the vehicle, otherwise the mechanical part may turn while the anti-theft system still refuses to recognize it.

Avoiding a second visit also means matching the repair to the hardware in front of you. Sometimes extraction and a correct new key are enough. Sometimes the smarter call is cylinder service, rekeying, or replacing a failing component before it snaps another blade. A stiff storefront mortise lock, a residential deadbolt that only turns when the door is pulled tight, and a vehicle ignition with clear wear patterns do not all get the same answer. The goal is a key that enters cleanly, turns without excess force, and works consistently in every lock it is supposed to operate. That extra attention is what keeps a broken key from becoming a repeat problem.

What to have ready

Before you call, it helps to look at the lock and the broken piece in plain terms. Is the key snapped in a house deadbolt, a knob lock, a storefront cylinder, a padlock, an ignition, or a car door? If you have the part that came out in your hand, keep it. That broken head or bow can tell us the keyway, how worn the cuts are, and whether the blade likely failed from metal fatigue, twisting under bind, or a badly copied key that never matched the lock quite right. If the lock is on a vehicle, have the year, make, model, and whether you use a transponder key, flip key, fob with an emergency insert, or a push-button system with a hidden mechanical key. If the lock is on a home or business door, check whether there is one lock involved or several keyed alike.

A few quick checks also help avoid surprises on the day. Don't spray glue, force another key in behind the broken blade, or keep turning the plug if the lock already feels tight. Those steps often wedge the fragment deeper, bend wafers or pins, or turn a simple extraction into a lock repair. If the lock has been sticking, note when that started and whether the problem changes with weather, door alignment, or a heavy handle set. On a commercial door, say if it has a mortise cylinder, rim cylinder, panic hardware, or an Adams Rite style narrow-stile setup. On a car, mention if the key broke while turning in the ignition, while unlocking the door, or after the key was already cracked near the shoulder.

Have proof that you're the owner or authorized user of the property or vehicle. For a car, that usually means photo ID plus registration or insurance showing the VIN. For a house or business, be ready with ID and a document tying you to the address, or have the property manager or tenant contact available if needed. If another entrance is open, say so. If the broken key is the only working key you had, mention that too, because extraction and making a key that works again are related but not always identical jobs. Sometimes the lock is sound and only the key failed. Other times the key broke because the cylinder is worn, dirty, or out of alignment and needs attention before a new key will behave properly.

On the day, the first step is usually a close inspection of the lock face, the position of the fragment, and the condition of the cylinder or ignition. If the blade can be removed cleanly, the next step is checking whether the lock itself is still operating as it should. From there, the technician can cut a working key to match the lock or, if wear inside the lock has changed the way it reads the cuts, discuss whether repair or replacement makes more sense. For vehicle work near US-40, having the car parked where the door or ignition can be reached safely makes the process smoother. If the lock is on a business in downtown Elkton, letting staff know which entrance is affected can prevent a lot of confusion while the work is being done.

Frequently asked questions

If part of the broken key is still sticking out, should I try to pull it out myself?

Usually, no. If the key is sticking out enough to grab, it can feel tempting to use pliers or tweezers, but that often twists the blade deeper into the cylinder or damages the keyway. Lubricants can also make things worse if the lock is already dirty or worn. The safest move is to stop using the lock, leave the piece where it is, and have it extracted with the proper tools.

After you remove the broken piece, will the lock still work or does it usually need to be replaced?

It depends on why the key snapped. If the break happened because the key was weak or already cracked, the lock may work normally once the fragment is removed and the cylinder is cleared. If the lock was binding, misaligned, or badly worn, extraction alone may not solve the problem. In those cases, the lock may need repair, rekeying, or replacement to keep it from happening again.

Can you make a working key if all I have is the broken key and no spare?

Often, yes, but not in every case. If both pieces of the broken key are available and the cuts are readable, a new key can usually be made from that pattern. If part of the key is missing, bent beyond use, or worn so badly that the cuts are hard to trust, the better option may be to decode the lock or cut a key by the lock itself. The goal is a key that turns smoothly, not a rough copy.

Why do keys snap in locks in the first place, and how can I keep it from happening again?

Most broken keys come from a mix of wear and resistance. Old keys get thin at the grooves, and locks that are dirty, dry, or slightly out of alignment make people use more force than they should. Heavy keychains can also stress an ignition key over time. If a key starts bending, sticking, or needing a jiggle, stop using it. Recut or replace the key early, and have the lock checked before it turns into a bigger problem.
