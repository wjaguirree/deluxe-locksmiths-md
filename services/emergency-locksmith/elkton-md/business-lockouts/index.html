A business lockout usually starts at the lock, not the door. On a storefront, side entry, or office suite, the problem is often a worn cylinder, a damaged latch, a misaligned strike, or a key that turns but won't retract the bolt. We look at the lock as a system, because forcing one part can damage the rest of it.

The goal is to get the door open without turning a simple lockout into a full replacement. Business Lockout Guidance

If you're locked out, the first thing to do is stay with the site and take a quick look at the basics. Check whether there's a side door, rear entrance, or another point of access that may already be open, and see whether a manager, landlord, or coworker has a spare key stored on-site or nearby. If the office is in a shared building near historic Main Street or along one of the busier corridors into town, make sure the lockout is just for your suite and not the whole property. It also helps to have the door type, lock style, and any access control details ready when you call, along with a clear explanation of what happened before the lockout.

What you should leave alone matters just as much. Don't force the knob, pry the door, or keep trying a key that won't turn, since that can damage the latch, cylinder, or frame and turn a simple lockout into a bigger repair. If you have a card reader, keypad, or panic hardware, don't start taking parts off the door unless you already know that system well. We handle the lockout on-site and work to get you back inside without causing extra damage, which helps you get back to customers, staff, and the rest of the day's business. If there are breakable storefront panes, alarm sensors, or interior glass near the entrance, keep everyone clear of the door until we arrive.

If you own the business, the lockout is usually about keeping the front door, stockroom, or office secure without shutting the whole operation down. We focus on the hardware that failed and make sure the door still closes and locks correctly after the opening. If a key was lost, we can also talk through rekeying so the old key won't keep working.

If you're a tenant, the next step depends on the lease and who controls the hardware. Some spaces use landlord-owned master key systems, and some use cylinders that can be changed without replacing the full lock. For a business, the concern is broader: customer access, employee entry, and protecting inventory or records. A clean opening matters, but so does getting the right lock setup back in place after the door is open.

Business Lockouts in Elkton - common questions

My shop door in Elkton turns halfway and stops. Is that usually the lock or the door?

It can be either, but a halfway turn often points to the lock body, latch, or strike not lining up. If the door has shifted, the key may feel stiff even when the cylinder is fine. We check the hardware first, then the fit of the door and frame so the same problem doesn't keep coming back.

What should I have ready when you come out for a business lockout on Main Street?

Have proof you're allowed to be there, plus any keys, badges, or alarm codes that help confirm the space. If the business uses master keys, it helps to know who controls them. If there's a second entrance, loading door, or rear office door, tell us about it so we can choose the cleanest way in.

I'm worried the lock will be ruined if the door has to be opened. Is that likely?

Not usually. A business lockout does not automatically mean the lock has to be drilled or destroyed. If the issue is wear, a broken internal part, or a lost key, we try to open the door in a way that protects the hardware. If the lock is already damaged, we'll explain what needs replacement before changing anything.

Should I replace the whole lock after a lockout, or just the cylinder?

That depends on what failed. If the key control is the issue, a cylinder change or rekey may be enough. If the latch, case, or internal parts are worn out, the full lock may be the better fix. We look at how the door is used, how old the hardware is, and whether parts are still holding alignment.

What if it's a tenant space and the landlord says the lock belongs to them?

That's common in commercial buildings. We can assess whether the existing hardware can be opened and restored without changing ownership-controlled parts. If replacement is needed, we can usually match the door's setup and keep the conversation focused on the failing component, so the landlord and tenant both know what changed and why.
