Rekey or replace? How to tell which one you need

Two different problems with two different answers, and people routinely pay for the wrong one.

Call (443) 545-2775 Request service

By the Deluxe Locksmith team August 17, 2026

Two different problems with two different answers, and people routinely pay for the wrong one.

Rekey when the hardware is fine and the keys are the problem

Most of the time, the lock itself is not the problem. The key history is. That's what rekeying is for. If you bought a house, took over a rental, had staff turnover, lost a key, ended a relationship, or gave a contractor a key that never made its way back, the question is simple: do you still trust every copy that might be out there? If the lock works smoothly and the door is securing the way it should, changing the keying is usually the right move. You're not fixing broken hardware. You're cutting off old access.

This is the most common situation by a wide margin. People often assume they need all new locks after a move, but the real issue is that they have no way to know who still has a working key. The seller may have handed over two copies, but there could be extras with a neighbor, a dog walker, a former roommate, or a family member. Same idea at a business after an employee leaves. If one person had a key and you can't account for every copy, you have a security problem even if the lock is working perfectly.

We see the same thing after a breakup or after repair work on a house. Nobody wants to accuse anybody of bad intentions, but security is not about guessing people's motives. It's about control. Rekeying resets that control so the old keys stop working and only the new keys do. If you're in Cecil County and you just closed on a house, or a storefront off Pulaski Highway changed hands, this is usually the first lock decision to make. Not because the lock failed, but because the trust attached to the old key is gone.

Replace when the hardware itself is the problem

If the lock itself is worn out, damaged, or wrong for the job, rekeying won't solve the real problem. Rekeying only changes which key operates the cylinder. It does not repair sloppy internal parts, a weak latch, a cracked tailpiece, a bent bolt, or corrosion that's already eating into the mechanism. If the hardware is failing mechanically, the right answer is replacement, because the problem is no longer key control. It's reliability and security.

The signs are usually pretty clear once you know what to look for. If the key has to be jiggled every time, if it sticks going in or coming out, if the lock turns roughly even with a good key, or if the deadbolt only throws when you push, pull, or lift the door, something is wearing out or out of alignment. Rust around the cylinder, a faceplate that's loose, a latch that doesn't spring back cleanly, or a deadbolt that drags are all signs the hardware is past the point where a pin change helps. We also see locks that were fine years ago but are now the wrong grade for the traffic they get. A light-duty knob lock on a busy side door in Elkton or a storefront entrance near Route 40 may technically still work, but it wears fast because it was never meant for that use.

Replacement is also the right move when the type of lock no longer fits what you need. Maybe you need a proper deadbolt instead of relying on a keyed knob, panic hardware instead of a standard lever, or a weather-rated lock because corrosion keeps coming back. In those cases, keeping the old hardware and rekeying it just preserves the mismatch. We look at how the lock is built, how the door closes, and how the opening is actually used, because a lock should fit the door and the job, not just accept a new key.

How rekeying actually works

Rekeying changes the lock's working combination without changing the lock you see on the door. We take the cylinder out of the knob, lever, or deadbolt and open it up on the bench. Inside that cylinder is a stack of small pins that line up to one specific key cut. We replace or rearrange those pins so the old key no longer lines them up, then we cut a new key to match the new pin pattern. After that, the cylinder goes back into the same lock body and the lock works like it always did, just with a different key.

That's why rekeying leaves the visible hardware alone. The trim, finish, and shape on the door stay the same because we are not swapping the whole deadbolt or knob set. If your satin nickel hardware matches the rest of the house, or your storefront hardware is mounted and working properly, there is no reason to disturb it just to stop an old key from working. The change happens in the part that reads the key, not in the parts you grab with your hand.

It is also quicker than full replacement because there is less to take apart and less to fit back onto the door. A replacement means removing the existing hardware, making sure the new parts match the door prep, latch backset, strike alignment, and screw spacing, then testing all of it together. With a rekey, the door usually keeps the same hardware and the same fit. We are changing the lock's internal code, not rebuilding the door. For a lot of homes and businesses around Elkton and up toward Route 40, that is the right fix when the issue is the key access, not the lock itself.

How many times can a lock be rekeyed

There isn't a set number. A good lock cylinder can be rekeyed over and over as long as the core parts are still in solid shape. Rekeying changes the pin setup inside the plug so a different key works. That process doesn't automatically "use up" a lock. What matters is the condition of the plug, the keyway, and the small parts that have to move cleanly every time you turn the key. If those surfaces are still sound, we can usually rekey it again.

What actually wears out is metal. Keys rubbing in the keyway over the years can round things off. The plug can get sloppy inside the cylinder. Pins and springs can wear, stick, or stop lining up the way they should. Dirt, old lubricant, weather, and a badly worn key all speed that up. That's why two locks of the same age can be very different: one may still rekey nicely, while the other has enough internal wear that changing the pins won't fix the real problem.

The signs are pretty practical. If the key has to be jiggled just right, if turning feels rough even with the correct key, if the plug has noticeable play, or if the key goes in and out loosely, the cylinder may be nearing the end of what rekeying can solve. Another red flag is a lock that still acts up after it's cleaned and a proper key is cut. At that point the issue usually isn't the key combination anymore; it's wear in the hardware itself.

That's why we inspect the cylinder before recommending anything, whether we're at a house off Pulaski Highway or a storefront near downtown Elkton. People often ask, "Can this be rekeyed again?" The honest answer is yes, many times, until the lock starts showing wear that affects how it operates. Then replacement becomes the reliable fix, not because of a number, but because the metal has had enough.

Getting several locks onto one key

If you're tired of carrying one key for the front door, another for the side door, and a third for the deadbolt in the garage entry, we can often set those locks up so one key works across the house. That's called keyed alike. It's practical because you stop guessing which key goes where, and it cuts down on the extra copies floating around on keyrings, in junk drawers, and with relatives or dog walkers. For a normal home setup, this works best when the locks are the same brand and use the same keyway, which is the shape of the keyway slot and the matching key blank.

This is where people get tripped up. Two locks can look similar on the door and still not be able to take the same key. If one lock is Kwikset and the other is Schlage, they usually won't key alike because the internal parts and keyways are different. Even within one manufacturer, some product lines use different keyways, so one Schlage key may not fit another Schlage lock. If you've got a mix of old hardware, builder-grade knobs, and a newer deadbolt added later, we check what's actually there before saying yes. Sometimes the answer is simple rekeying. Sometimes one lock has to be replaced if your goal is one key across everything.

There's also a real security trade-off, and it's worth saying plainly. One key for every exterior door is convenient, but if that one key is lost, stolen, or copied without your knowledge, it opens every lock in that keyed-alike group. With separate keys, a lost key may only affect one door. Some households are fine with that trade because daily use matters more. Others would rather keep a detached garage, rental space, or back entry on a different key. Around Elkton and out toward Route 40, we usually tell people to think about who has copies, not just how handy one-key access sounds.

Restricted keys, and who can copy your key

If your key is a standard house or office key, anyone holding it can usually get a copy made at a hardware store or kiosk. The machine does not know whether that person is the owner, a tenant, an employee, or someone who borrowed the key for an afternoon. It just reads the cuts and duplicates them. That matters because a lot of people think rekeying alone controls copies. It does not. Rekeying changes which key works the lock. It does not stop that new key from being copied if it is an ordinary keyway.

A restricted keyway is different. Those keys and blanks are controlled, so duplication is limited to authorized holders through the locksmith or system owner. The point is not that the key is magical. The point is control. If you manage rentals, employee access, maintenance staff, cleaners, or a shared building with several people carrying keys, restricted keys give you a real chain of permission. You know who is allowed to order more keys, and random copies are much harder to make. For a property manager with units off Pulaski Highway, or a business with staff turnover, that solves a problem ordinary keys do not.

A lot of homeowners do not need that level of control. If it is your own house, only a couple of family members have keys, and you are not handing them out to contractors or rotating occupants, a standard key is often fine. Restricted keys make sense when key copies themselves are the risk. They are not automatically better for every front door in Elkton. The right question is simple: are you trying to control who can open the lock, or who can create more keys. Those are two different problems, and they need different answers.

Grades, and what they actually mean for your door

A lock grade is basically a shorthand for how much use and abuse a lock is built to take, and how well it stands up when someone tries to force it. That matters, but it gets oversold. A better grade does not turn every lock into a fortress, and it does not mean every door in the house needs the toughest hardware made. What it should tell you is whether the lock fits the job. For a front door that gets used all day, faces weather, and is the main point of entry, you want hardware meant for repeated use and solid resistance. For a bedroom, pantry, or hall closet, that would be overkill because the job is privacy or simple latching, not exterior security.

The reason this matters is that people often buy based on the package instead of the door. A strong lock on a weak door frame is not a strong setup. On the other hand, putting light-duty hardware on a front entry that gets slammed, tugged, and used constantly is asking for early wear. If your exterior door is on a busy entrance off Pulaski Highway, it needs more from the lock than an interior office door does. Good hardware should match the traffic, the exposure, and the consequences if it fails.

What should you ask for? Ask whether the lock is suited for exterior use, whether it is built for heavy daily cycling, and whether the deadbolt and latch have solid metal parts instead of the light stuff that wears out fast. Ask whether your strike plate and screws are part of the security picture, because they are. And ask us to look at the whole door, not just the cylinder. The right answer is not always the highest grade on the shelf. It is the grade that makes sense for that specific door and how you actually use it.

When it is a business, think in systems not locks

On a business door, the real question usually isn't "Is this lock bad?" It's "Who should be able to get into which areas, and what changes when staffing changes?" A storefront, office, warehouse, or medical space often has a front door, interior offices, storage rooms, back entries, and maybe a gate or equipment room. Not everyone should have the same access to all of it. If you treat each cylinder like a separate problem, you end up with a ring full of keys, doors nobody is fully tracking, and a lot of confusion the first time an employee leaves or a manager changes.

That is where a master key system makes sense. Instead of thinking one door at a time, we map access by role. A front desk employee may need the main entry and supply closet. A supervisor may need those plus office doors. Ownership may need one master key that opens the whole site. The point is control. You can give people the access they need to do their job without handing out a key that opens everything. That matters even more in a multi-tenant building, a shop off Pulaski Highway, or any place where deliveries, shift changes, and shared spaces are part of the day.

Staff turnover is usually what exposes a bad key setup. If someone leaves and nobody is sure which doors their key opens, you do not have a lock problem first. You have a key control problem. A planned system gives you a clear answer: this person had access to these areas, and now that access needs to be changed. That is why commercial work should start with a simple access plan, not a pile of duplicate keys. We help businesses in places like Elkton set that up so the hardware supports the way the business actually runs, instead of fighting it.
