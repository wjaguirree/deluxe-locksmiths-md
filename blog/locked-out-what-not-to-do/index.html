Locked out? Four things not to do, and one to do first

The damage is usually done in the short window before the locksmith is called, not by the lockout itself.

By the Deluxe Locksmith team August 15, 2026

The damage is usually done in the short window before the locksmith is called, not by the lockout itself.

Do not force a window

If you're locked out, don't start leaning on a window, prying at the sash, or thinking one hard hit will solve it. Glass breaks in ugly ways, and even when it doesn't shatter, the damage usually spreads to the parts around it. We see bent tracks, cracked vinyl, split wood, torn screens, and latches ripped out of alignment. A lockout is one problem. A damaged window turns it into a repair job that follows you after the door is open.

The part most people don't expect is the frame damage. Once a window frame gets forced, it often never sits square again. That means drafts, water getting where it should not, and a sash that sticks or will not close the way it used to. On older homes around Elkton, especially where the frames have already seen years of swelling and settling, that damage can be permanent. People think they are saving themselves trouble by using the window. In practice, it is one of the most common ways a simple lockout becomes an insurance claim.

There is also the security issue afterward. A window that no longer locks properly is not a small detail you deal with later. If the latch is bent, the keeper pulls loose, or the frame no longer lines up, you have created a second entry point that may not be secure that night. Even if the glass is intact, a weakened frame can make the window much easier to force the next time, by you or by someone else. If you are standing outside frustrated in a driveway off Pulaski Highway, it can feel like the fastest option. It usually creates more damage than the original problem and leaves the house less secure once you get back in.

Do not attack the lock with tools

A locked door often stays a lockout problem until someone starts digging at the keyway with a screwdriver or grabbing the cylinder with pliers. That's when it turns into lock damage. The pins, springs, plug, and face of the cylinder are small parts built to line up precisely. A screwdriver spreads the keyway, burrs the inside, and snaps off pieces that fall deeper into the lock. Pliers on the outside usually do the same kind of harm from the other direction. Instead of opening a working lock, we arrive to a lock that has been twisted, crushed, or jammed solid.

That matters because a lock that could have been opened cleanly may now have to be drilled out and replaced. Even if the door itself is fine, a chewed-up cylinder gives us less to work with. If the plug is distorted or the keyway is packed with broken metal, the non-destructive option disappears. People are usually trying to help themselves, but the force they use is exactly what takes a repairable lock and makes it unrecoverable.

It also closes off another good option after entry: rekeying. If the cylinder is still intact, we can often change the pins so the old key no longer works and issue a new key for the same hardware. That keeps the lock matched to your door and avoids changing out parts that were otherwise serviceable. Once the cylinder has been wrecked with tools, rekeying is off the table because there is no sound cylinder left to pin. If you're standing outside your home in Elkton or coming back to a storefront off Pulaski Highway, put the tools down before you make the lock itself the bigger problem.

The card trick does not work on your door

That trick comes from a very specific kind of door: a spring latch, no deadbolt thrown, and enough slop in the fit between the door and frame to slide a flexible card in at the right angle. On that setup, the card can press the beveled latch back into the door. That's the whole idea. If your door has a deadbolt engaged, the card does nothing, because a deadbolt is a solid bar projecting into the frame. There is no spring to push back. On a tighter, better-fitted door, there usually isn't enough space to reach the latch properly anyway.

On most modern exterior doors, trying it just damages things. People end up bending the latch, chewing up the weatherstripping, cracking the card off inside the gap, or scratching the door edge and frame. Then the door still won't open, but now the latch may not move smoothly even after we get there. We see this a lot on doors with reinforced strike plates or doors that have settled a little. The card catches, twists, and forces pressure onto parts that were never meant to be used that way.

The videos are misleading because they're usually made on the easiest possible door, often already loose, already unlatched, and filmed from the best angle with several tries cut out. You don't see the failed attempts, and you usually can't tell whether the deadbolt is actually off. A quick clip makes it look universal when it's really a narrow trick for a narrow situation. If you're standing outside a house in Cecil County or near downtown Elkton with a locked exterior door, assume the card trick is more likely to waste effort and add damage than solve the problem.

Where you wait matters more than the lock

If you're locked out beside traffic or outside a building after dark, stop thinking about the door for a second and think about where you're standing. A locked car in a parking lot is one problem. A locked car on the shoulder of Route 40 is a different problem. The risk is not the lock. It's poor visibility, distracted drivers, and being stuck in a spot where nobody can tell whether you need help. If you can move safely, get to a place that's lit, open, and easy for other people to see. That usually means near a storefront, a gas station, a lobby, or under working exterior lights, not tucked behind a dumpster or down the side of a building.

If the vehicle is in a dangerous spot, don't stand between it and traffic, and don't lean into the road trying to wave someone down. Stay well off the travel lane. If you can safely reach a safer area nearby, do that first and keep the vehicle in sight if possible. If the area feels active and public, staying near the car often makes sense because it helps us find you and helps you keep an eye on the vehicle. If the area feels isolated, badly lit, or you're outside alone late in the evening, your safety matters more than staying next to the door. Step into a nearby business or another visible public place and call from there.

The reason we give this advice is simple: lockouts turn into bigger problems when people freeze in a bad spot or stay hidden because they're embarrassed. We'd much rather have you calm, visible, and easy to locate than standing in a dark corner focused on the handle. If you call us from somewhere safer, tell us exactly where the vehicle or building entrance is and where you'll be waiting so we can meet you without confusion.

What a locksmith will ask you to prove

Before we open anything, we need to know you have the right to be there. That is not us being difficult. It protects your property, your family, your landlord if you rent, and our technicians as well. A lockout can look the same whether it's an honest mistake or someone trying to get into a place or car that isn't theirs. Proof of entitlement is the line that keeps a stressful situation from turning into the wrong kind of call.

For a home, the simplest proof is usually a government photo ID with the same address as the house. If your ID still shows an old address, that does not automatically stop things. Mail, a utility bill, closing paperwork, or another document that ties your name to that property can help. If you're locked out of a rental, we may ask for a lease, rent portal information, or for the property manager or landlord to confirm that you live there. That protects tenants too. It helps make sure a former roommate, ex, or unauthorized guest is not using a locksmith to get past the door.

For a vehicle, registration, insurance, or title with your name on it is usually what we're looking for, along with photo ID. If those papers are inside the car, tell us that up front. That's common, and we can usually verify once the vehicle is open. If the car is a company vehicle, a work ID or a call from the business may be needed. None of this is an obstacle put in your way. It's normal, responsible practice. If you can, have your ID and any paperwork ready while you wait, whether you're at home in Elkton or parked off Pulaski Highway. It makes the job smoother and keeps the right person on the right side of the lock.

What non-destructive entry actually means

Most lockouts do not require breaking anything. The job is to get the door open and leave the lock, the door, and the frame working the same way they did before you were shut out. That matters because a lock is only one part of the opening. If the latch gets bent, the strike gets torn loose, or the frame gets spread, you can end up with a door that closes badly, drags, or will not secure properly even after the key is back in your hand. Non-destructive entry means we start with methods meant to defeat the lock or the latch without damaging the hardware around it.

Why that works so often is simple: most lockouts are access problems, not broken-door problems. A key is inside, a knob got locked, a deadbolt was thrown, or the latch shut behind you. If the lock is in decent shape, there is usually a clean way in. We check what type of cylinder or latch you have, how the door is handing, and whether the hardware is aligned normally. A front door in Elkton that was working fine before the lockout should, in most cases, work fine after we open it.

There are exceptions, and it is better to be straight about them. Some high-security cylinders are designed to resist the usual opening methods. In other cases, the lock was already failing, packed with debris, misaligned, or partly damaged from prior force. Then the cleanest option can be to sacrifice the cylinder to protect the rest of the door. That means the removable core of the lock is drilled or otherwise taken out so the door can be opened without wrecking the door slab or frame. After that, we replace the cylinder or the affected lock hardware, test the latch and deadbolt, and make sure the door locks and unlocks correctly before we leave.

The lockout scam to watch for

One pattern shows up over and over in lockouts. The person on the phone gives a neat, reassuring quote before they've asked the basic questions that matter: what kind of door, what kind of lock, whether the key is inside, whether anything is broken already. Then an unmarked vehicle pulls up, not a van or car that clearly belongs to a locksmith business. Once they're at the door, the number changes and the answer to everything is, "We have to drill it." That is the part to watch. Drilling is fast for the wrong operator because it turns a simple opening into a lock replacement, and that shifts the job in their favor, not yours.

A legitimate locksmith works the other way around. We ask enough over the phone to understand what we're coming to, because the door and hardware tell us a lot about the likely entry method. When we arrive, we identify who we are and look at the lock before touching it. If a lock can be opened without destroying it, that is usually the first route. Drilling is a last resort, not the opening move, because once metal is cut there is no undo button. You're no longer dealing with a lockout alone; now you're dealing with parts, rekeying, and a door that may not secure properly until the whole job is finished.

The reason this scam works is simple: most people are stressed and standing outside their own door, whether that's at a townhouse off Pulaski Highway or a storefront in Elkton. Stress makes vague answers sound good enough. Slow the process down just enough to ask direct questions: What are you driving, what company are you with, and why do you believe drilling is necessary on this specific lock. A real locksmith can answer that plainly.

After the door is open

Getting back inside solves the immediate problem, but it doesn't always solve the reason it happened. A lockout is often a warning. If your keys were lost or taken, the smart move is to have the lock re-coded on the same visit. That changes the lock to work with a different key, so the missing key no longer fits. People sometimes put that off because the door is open and life moves on. Then the lost key turns up in the wrong hands, or it stays out there and nags at you every time you leave the house.

The same goes for a lock or door that was already giving you trouble. If you had to jiggle the key, lift the handle, shove the door, or pull it just right before this happened, that problem did not fix itself. It will usually do the same thing again, and often at the worst time. In a lot of homes around Cecil County, the lock gets blamed when the real issue is alignment: the latch isn't meeting the strike cleanly, the deadbolt is binding, or the door has shifted enough that everything feels stiff. Forcing it every day wears the key, the cylinder, and the hardware around it.

That's why we tell people to use the open door as a chance to sort the root cause while we're there. If the lock is worn, we can tell you whether it needs service or replacement. If the strike needs adjustment, the cylinder is loose, the key is badly copied, or the closer is pulling the door out of position, those are worth fixing before you lock up again. The goal is not just to get you in. It's to leave you with a door that works cleanly the next time you step out, whether you're in Elkton or heading home after a stop off Route 40.
