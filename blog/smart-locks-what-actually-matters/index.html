Smart locks: what actually matters when you buy one

The app is the least important part. The mechanical fit decides whether you like it.

Call (443) 545-2775 Request service

By the Deluxe Locksmith team August 13, 2026

The app is the least important part. The mechanical fit decides whether you like it.

A smart lock is still a lock

Before the app, the keypad, or the brand name, the lock has to fit the door correctly. That starts with the bore hole and the backset. If the hole size is off, or the backset doesn't match the lock body, the hardware sits wrong from the start. On some doors, the lock can be made to mount, but it binds just enough to cause trouble later. A regular deadbolt might still limp along because your hand can muscle it through. A smart lock's motor can't do that. It expects the parts to line up and move cleanly every time.

The strike matters just as much. The bolt should slide into the strike opening fully, without rubbing the edge, lifting the door, or needing you to push, pull, or twist the knob to help it. If you have to lean on the door to lock it, that's not a smart-lock problem. That's an alignment problem between the door, the frame, and the strike. We see this a lot on doors that have settled, doors that swell in damp weather, and doors where the old deadbolt "worked" only because the user had gotten used to forcing it.

That's why people often install a smart lock, then decide the electronics are unreliable. The motor stalls, the lock beeps, or it says locked when the bolt didn't throw all the way. What's really happening is mechanical resistance. The lock is trying to do a deadbolt's job on a door that was already fighting the deadbolt. Around Cecil County and up near Route 40, a lot of that comes down to older doors, seasonal movement, and strike plates that were never set quite right. If the bolt can't extend smoothly by hand first, adding electronics won't fix it. It usually exposes the problem.

Check the door before you check the reviews

A smart lock can only work with the door it's bolted to. If your current deadbolt already needs a shoulder bump, a hard twist, or a shove on the door to lock, that's the problem to solve first. The motor inside a smart lock has nowhere near the force your hand and wrist do. You can muscle a sticky deadbolt into place and barely notice it. The lock's motor can't. That's why people end up saying a smart lock is unreliable when the real issue is a door that's slightly out of alignment.

You can test this yourself before you buy anything. Open the door and throw the deadbolt in and out a few times. It should move smoothly, with no scraping, catching, or extra effort. Then do the same test with the door closed. Pay attention to the difference. If it turns easily with the door open but gets tight when the door is shut, the bolt and strike aren't lining up cleanly. That means the lock is pushing the bolt against the edge of the hole or rubbing the frame instead of sliding straight in.

That small bit of drag matters more than app features or star ratings. A smart lock is doing the same job over and over with limited battery power and a small motor. If the bolt has to fight the frame each time, you get failed locks, partial locks, fast battery drain, and the kind of behavior people call "glitchy." In a place that sees humidity swings like Cecil County, doors move a little through the year, so a setup that is barely working now usually gets worse, not better.

If the test shows resistance, fix the fit first. Sometimes that means adjusting the strike, tightening loose hardware, correcting sag at the hinges, or dealing with weatherstrip pressure. Once the deadbolt works smoothly with the door closed, a smart lock has a fair shot at working the way it should.

Keyed override matters more than people think

Dead batteries almost never happen at a convenient time. It's usually late, your hands are full, the weather is bad, or the lock has been giving a low-battery warning that got ignored for a while. That's why we tell people not to get distracted by the app and forget the backup plan. A smart lock that still has a real key cylinder gives you a simple way in when the electronics quit. If there's no key override, the next best thing is external contacts on the outside of the lock so you can touch a battery to it long enough to wake it up and unlock the door.

That matters because a dead battery should be an annoyance, not a lockout. With a keyed override, the lock can fail in one system without trapping you outside. The mechanical side still works, and that separation is valuable. It also helps if the keypad acts up, the touchscreen stops responding in cold weather, or the lock loses power faster than expected. We see plenty of doors around Elkton where the owner assumed "battery powered" meant "problem free" until the first bad day proved otherwise.

The trade-off with keypad-only designs is mostly about convenience and appearance. Some people like the cleaner look, and some prefer not to carry a house key at all. But that cleaner look usually means you're trusting one system to do everything. If the battery dies and there's no external jump option, your only plan is hoping another entrance is open or calling for help. That's fine on paper and frustrating in real life, especially if the only door you use is the front one off Pulaski Highway.

If you want fewer surprises, pick a model with a physical key override first. If you don't want a key cylinder, make sure the lock has external emergency power contacts and that you know how they work before you need them.

Batteries, cold and the real-world lifespan

Most smart-lock battery problems come from three things: the motor turning the lock, the radio waking up to talk, and the cold. The motor is the heavy draw. Every lock and unlock takes a burst of power to move the bolt or clutch. If the door is a little tight, or the bolt rubs because the alignment is off, the motor has to work harder every cycle and the batteries go faster. The radio matters too. Bluetooth usually sips power. Wi-Fi uses more because the lock has to reach farther and check in more often. A lock that's constantly reconnecting because of weak signal can burn through batteries a lot quicker than the same lock on a stable setup.

Winter changes the whole picture, especially on an exterior door. Batteries don't like cold, and a lock on a front door sees that temperature swing every day. A set of cells that seems fine indoors can act weak outside once the air turns cold off the Chesapeake. You'll notice it first as a slower motor, a delayed response, or a lock that works fine in the afternoon and struggles early in the morning. Cold also makes any sticky door or stiff weatherstripping more obvious, so the lock needs more force right when the batteries can deliver less.

Don't ignore a low-battery warning. Smart locks usually give notice before they quit, but they don't all fail gracefully. Some keep enough power for a few more cycles, then suddenly won't motor at all. That's why we tell people to replace the batteries as soon as the warning shows up, not after the weekend or after one more trip out through Route 40 wind and cold. Keep the exact battery type your lock calls for in the house, and use good fresh cells from the same brand and batch. Mixing old and new batteries, or using the wrong chemistry, is asking for flaky behavior that looks like a lock problem but is really a power problem.

Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and hubs, in plain English

Bluetooth is the simple version. Your phone talks to the lock when you're standing at the door or close by. That usually means better battery life, because the lock is not trying to stay connected to your home network all day. It also means less to set up. If what you want is keyless entry, phone unlock, and maybe codes for family, Bluetooth often does the job without adding much complexity. The tradeoff is reach. If you're at work, away for the weekend, or just upstairs and out of range, you usually can't check the lock or change anything until you're back near it.

Wi-Fi is the opposite. It lets the lock talk through your internet, so you can lock it, unlock it, or see its status from anywhere with signal. That's useful if deliveries matter to you, if a dog walker or cleaner comes when you're out, or if you regularly wonder whether you locked up after leaving the house. The reason Wi-Fi uses more power is simple: the lock has to work harder to stay in touch with the network. That doesn't make it bad. It just means the convenience is tied to a heavier battery load and a setup that depends more on your router, signal strength, and app working properly.

Hub-based systems sit between those two. The lock uses a lower-power connection at the door, and a hub inside the house handles the long-distance communication. In real life, that can be a good fit for a larger house, a detached garage entry, or a front door that doesn't get strong Wi-Fi on its own. It can also make sense if you already use a broader smart-home system. If your household wants the fewest moving parts, Bluetooth is often enough. If remote control is the whole point, Wi-Fi makes sense. If you want remote access without asking the lock itself to do all the work, a hub is worth a look.

Codes are the real advantage over a key

The part people end up using every day isn't the app screen. It's the keypad and the ability to give each person their own code. That matters more because it changes how you control access in real life. A family member can have one code, the cleaner another, a contractor another, and a guest their own short-term code. If somebody loses a code, changes jobs, or no longer needs to come by, you remove that one code instead of changing the whole setup or getting new keys made for everybody.

That only works if you set it up the right way. Don't give multiple people the same code just because it's easy to remember. If the cleaner and the dog walker both use 2580, you have no idea who came in, and if one person should lose access, you end up shutting out both. Separate codes let you keep things clean and simple. They also help you avoid the common problem where an old worker, former tenant, or frequent guest still has a way in long after they should.

The other good habit is not reusing the same code everywhere. We see people use one favorite four-digit number for the front door, alarm, garage, and phone. That's convenient right up until somebody learns it. Treat door codes like house keys: specific to one place and one purpose. If your son needs entry after school, his code should be his code for that door, not the same number your contractor used last season or the same one you punch in at another property. That's the real practical advantage of a smart lock. Not better graphics in an app, but better control over who can enter and when.

What happens if the company disappears

A smart lock can outlive the company that made the app, and that's not a rare thought experiment. Brands get sold, apps stop getting updates, cloud services get shut down, and phones move on to new operating systems. That does not automatically turn the lock on your door into junk. The important question is simpler: if the app goes away, what still works at the door itself? On a good setup, the basic job of locking and unlocking still works from the keypad, the interior turn piece, and any local programming built into the lock. If those core functions depend on a server somewhere else, you're betting your front door on a business decision you don't control.

This is why we tell people to separate "smart features" from "door functions." Remote alerts, activity history, voice assistant links, and unlocking from another town all usually depend on the manufacturer's app and servers. If that service ends, those extras may disappear even though the hardware still has power. Local functions are different. A lock that stores codes in the lock itself and lets you manage at least the basics without an internet connection is usually the safer buy. It gives you a usable lock even if your Wi-Fi is out, your phone is replaced, or the brand stops supporting older models.

Around Cecil County, we've seen plenty of smart devices become ordinary devices after support fades, and that's not always a problem if the ordinary device still does the main job well. Before you buy, look for plain language in the manual about offline operation, local code storage, and whether initial setup or future changes require an account. If a lock needs the company's servers for everyday ownership, not just for extra features, that's a weaker long-term choice. The fewer outside systems your door depends on, the fewer ways it can let you down.

Renting, landlords and shared entrances

If you rent, start with permission before you buy anything. A lot of tenants assume a smart lock is like changing a shower head. Sometimes it is, sometimes it isn't. The landlord may have rules about matching hardware, fire code, master key systems, or what can be changed on an exterior door. Get approval in writing, and make sure the lock you choose can be removed without leaving extra holes, a changed strike, or visible marks on the door. Keep the original lock, screws, strike, and any trim in a labeled bag. That matters because moving out usually means putting the door back exactly the way you found it, not "close enough." If the old hardware gets lost, a simple swap turns into a hunt for parts that fit the old prep and finish.

For landlords, smart locks can make turnover easier, but only if you think past the app. The real advantage is controlling access between tenants without wondering how many copies of the old key are still out there. A good setup lets you end old user access cleanly, issue a fresh code for the next tenant, and keep a physical record of what hardware is on each unit. That only works if the lock is installed properly and the door closes and latches the way it should. If the fit is poor, your management gets harder, not easier, because every battery warning or failed lock event becomes a maintenance call.

Shared entrances are a separate problem. If you live in an apartment building, duplex, or converted house near Elkton where one outside door serves more than one occupant, that door usually isn't yours alone to change. It affects every resident, visitors, deliveries, emergency access, and sometimes the building's code compliance. Even in a small multi-unit setup off Pulaski Highway, the communal door needs a building-level plan, not one tenant's gadget choice. Your own unit door may be a good place for a smart lock. The shared entrance usually needs a different kind of access system altogether.
